## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. Gemini Robotics 2.5: robots that can fold pants and pack pastries

- **What happened:** On Nov 10, Google DeepMind announced Gemini Robotics 2.5, its newest vision-language-action model, which takes in camera images and instructions and outputs robot movements.
- **Key details:**
  - Tested on more than 200 tasks. DeepMind reports success rates 30% to 100% higher than the previous version, depending on the task, and 20% to 30% faster completion.
  - Some big jumps from Gemini Robotics 2: closing a toolbox lid went from 0% to 93%, packing pastries with tongs from 5% to 100%, untying ribbon bows from 0% to 70%.
  - In a pilot on Mercedes-Benz production lines in Hungary and Germany, Apptronik's Apollo 2 humanoid sorted car parts with a 96% success rate.
  - DeepMind also open-sourced **ASIMOV-Jailbreak**, a benchmark of adversarial prompts meant to trick a robot into unsafe actions. Its Gemini 4 Argon model resisted 99.5% of them, compared with 82% for the older Gemini Robotics ER 2.
- **Background:** A "jailbreak" is an input crafted to get a model to ignore its safety rules. With robots, the risk is a harmful physical action, not just harmful text.
- **The catch:** All numbers are self-reported, and the factory figure comes from a controlled proof of concept. The model is going only to DeepMind's robotics partners for now.
- **Why it matters for you:** Look at how the results are reported: per-task success rates, not one average. A single average would hide that some tasks are still at 40%. Break down your own evaluation results the same way.

## Research Highlights

### 3. Is your robot's memory just a clock? (arXiv:2611.14488)

- **What happened:** In a paper posted Nov 10, researchers at Nanyang Technological University showed that robot models with memory often learn to track *time passed* rather than *what has happened*.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Many robot models index their memory by timestep ("this happened at step 40"). During training, step count and task progress almost always go together, so the model learns to use the clock as a stand-in for progress.
  - When the robot pauses or gets delayed during deployment, the clock keeps ticking. The model reads that as progress and can stop early. The authors call this "phantom progress."
  - Their fix is to index memory by *events* (for example, "after the gripper closed") instead of time. It recovers much of the lost performance without retraining.
- **Key details:** With pauses added, success fell from 47 to 16 out of 50 trials on one task and from 44 to 10 on another.
- **Why it matters for you:** This is **shortcut learning**: a feature that predicts well in training only by coincidence. You'll see it in tabular data too, for example a "row ID" or "date" column that happens to line up with the label. Test your model on cases where the coincidence breaks.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14488)

### 4. Are detailed rewards worth the extra compute? Mostly not (arXiv:2611.13560)

- **What happened:** A Case Western Reserve University team posted a study on Nov 10 comparing two ways of giving rewards when training LLMs to reason with reinforcement learning.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Standard GRPO (a popular RL method for LLMs) gives one reward at the end: was the final answer right?
  - "Dense" rewards also score each intermediate step, often by sampling many continuations from that step to estimate how likely it is to lead to a right answer. That costs a lot of extra tokens.
  - The authors compared the two at **equal compute**, meaning the same total number of tokens generated.
- **Key details:**
  - 1,668 training runs across six models.
  - On Qwen2.5-1.5B, the dense-reward method ended 14.3 percentage points lower than plain GRPO at the same token budget.
  - One cheap trick did help a little: training on prompts the model gets right about half the time.
- **Why it matters for you:** "Method B beats method A" means little unless both had the same budget. When you compare models, match the compute, data or tuning time.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13560)

**Also noted:** [BioXArena-Pro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13994), a benchmark of 64 research-level biomedical ML tasks where the best of 22 AI agent setups scored 55.1 out of 100 · [Inherited Weights](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13441) finds that older tokenizers can need up to 10x more tokens for Amharic than for English, which makes the same request cost more.

## Tools & Libraries

### 5. Hugging Face adds "decision models" to Transformers

- **What happened:** On Nov 10, Hugging Face introduced decision models, a standard way to ask a model fixed-answer questions and get probabilities back instead of generated text.
- **Key details:**
  - You pass a **state** (such as a support ticket), some **questions** and the **allowed answers**. The model scores each allowed answer in a single forward pass and generates no text.
  - Three question types: **Choice** (pick one of several labels), **Score** (a position on an ordered scale like low/medium/high, reported as an expected value such as 1.7) and **Noul** (the probability that a yes/no statement is true).
  - New classes: `pipeline("decision")`, `AutoModelForDecision` and `DecisionConfig`. You can fine-tune with the usual `Trainer` and serve with `transformers serve` or llama.cpp. TRL support is still in progress.
  - Models on the Hub carry a `decision-model` tag. Some are small ModernBERT encoders and some are adapted from larger LLMs.
- **The catch:** Hugging Face notes the probabilities aren't automatically calibrated, so a 0.9 may not mean "right 90% of the time." No benchmarks were published.
- **Why it matters for you:** If you've asked a chatbot to "reply in JSON with one of these labels" and then had to deal with broken output, this is a cleaner way to do it. See today's LLM concept.
- **Source:** [Hugging Face blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/decision)

### 6. Smaller search indexes with token pooling in Sentence Transformers

- **What happened:** On Nov 11, Hugging Face published a training method and code that keep multi-vector search models accurate when their stored vectors are cut by about three-quarters.
- **Key details:**
  - Multi-vector models (ColBERT-style) store one vector per token of each document. That makes search accurate, but the index gets very large.
  - **Token pooling** groups similar token vectors with hierarchical clustering (today's ML concept) and keeps only the average of each group.
  - Pooling to a quarter of the vectors (about 74.8% fewer) kept 94.6% of an ordinary model's search quality. A model trained with pooling in mind kept 97.8% (51.23 vs 52.39 nDCG@10).
  - Checkpoints, built on ModernBERT-base and trained on 120,000 MS MARCO examples, are on the Hub, and the training and evaluation scripts are in Sentence Transformers. A GPU clustering kernel is reported as 30–150x faster than SciPy.
- **Background:** nDCG@10 scores how well the top 10 search results are ranked, with relevant results near the top scoring higher.
- **Why it matters for you:** Training a model in the same conditions it will run in (here, with pooling) is a general idea worth reusing. The same idea is behind quantization-aware training.
- **Source:** [Hugging Face blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/token-pooling)

**Also noted:** Databricks' November release notes include a `UCDataset` beta that streams Unity Catalog tables straight into PyTorch training, and Genie ZeroOps (beta, Nov 12), which watches data pipelines and suggests fixes when they break ([Databricks](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/release-notes/product/2026/november)).

### 8. Google launches two new TPUs, including one for sensitive data

- **What happened:** On Nov 9, Google announced two additions to its eighth-generation TPUs (Tensor Processing Units, its own AI chips): the TPU 8tx and the TPU 8i Max.
- **Key details:**
  - **TPU 8tx** is for confidential computing. Hardware isolation keeps data and model weights protected while they're in use. Google says it has up to 2x lower latency than the TPU 8t, with almost no performance cost for the security features.
  - **TPU 8i Max** puts two TPU 8i chips in one package, doubling compute, on-chip memory (SRAM) and memory bandwidth. It's aimed at very long context windows.
  - TorchTPU, open-sourced last month, lets PyTorch and Hugging Face code run on TPUs without rewrites. Google says nearly 100 customers now use TPUs.
- **The catch:** Performance claims are Google's own, and there's no pricing or availability date yet.
- **Why it matters for you:** Hospitals and banks often can't send data to a shared cloud chip, which has kept them from using large models. Hardware like the 8tx is meant to solve that, and it could open more ML jobs in regulated industries.
- **Source:** [Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/infrastructure-and-cloud/global-network/tpu-8i-max-and-8tx/)

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Hierarchical Clustering

**In one sentence:** Hierarchical clustering groups data by starting with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merging the two most similar clusters, which builds a tree of groupings (a **dendrogram**).

**Intuition:** Think of a family tree built in reverse. Siblings pair up first, then families join into extended families, then clans. You choose how many groups you want by cutting the tree at a chosen height.

**Formula / Tiny example:** **Ward's method** merges the pair of clusters that adds the least to the total within-cluster sum of squares (SSE). For clusters A and B, that increase is `(nA·nB / (nA+nB)) · (meanA − meanB)²`. Take five 1-D points: 1, 2, 6, 7, 15.
- Merge {1,2}: cost (1·1/2)·1² = 0.5. Merge {6,7}: also 0.5.
- {1,2} with {6,7}: (2·2/4)·(1.5−6.5)² = 25. {6,7} with {15}: (2/3)·8.5² ≈ 48.2. So {1,2,6,7} forms at cost 25.
- Last, {1,2,6,7} (mean 4) joins {15}: (4/5)·11² = 96.8. That big jump suggests the natural split: {1,2,6,7} and {15}.
```python
from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering
labels = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=2, linkage="ward").fit_predict([[1],[2],[6],[7],[15]])
```

**When it's used:** Grouping cells in single-cell genomics, building customer segments, organizing documents, and compressing token vectors (story 6).

**Common pitfalls:** Not scaling features first, so a column measured in dollars outweighs one measured in years. And running it on very large datasets: the basic algorithm needs all pairwise distances, which gets slow and memory-hungry past tens of thousands of points.

**In today's news:** Story 6 uses Ward clustering to merge similar token vectors into one.

### LLMs: Structured Outputs and Constrained Decoding

**In one sentence:** Constrained decoding forces an LLM's output to follow a set format, such as a fixed list of labels or a JSON schema, by blocking any token that would break the format.

**Intuition:** Compare an open-ended exam question with a multiple-choice one. With multiple choice, the student can't answer "purple" when the options are A–D. The grader no longer has to interpret messy answers.

**Formula / Tiny example:** At each step the model produces a score (logit) for every token. Constrained decoding sets the logit of every disallowed token to −∞, so after softmax its probability is 0. For a single label, you can just compare the label scores. Say a ticket gets logits Billing 2.0, Technical 0.5, Sales −1.0:
- e^2.0 = 7.389, e^0.5 = 1.649, e^−1.0 = 0.368; sum = 9.406
- P(Billing) = 0.786, P(Technical) = 0.175, P(Sales) = 0.039
- Hugging Face's "confidence" (story 5) rescales the top probability so a uniform guess scores 0 and certainty scores 1: (0.786 − 1/3) / (1 − 1/3) ≈ 0.68.
```python
import torch
logits = torch.tensor([2.0, 0.5, -1.0])   # scores for the 3 allowed labels only
probs = torch.softmax(logits, dim=0)       # tensor([0.7856, 0.1753, 0.0391])
```

**When it's used:** Classifying and routing tickets, extracting fields into JSON, calling tools (function calling) and anything where code downstream needs a guaranteed format.

**Common pitfalls:** A guaranteed format doesn't guarantee a correct answer: the model will always pick *a* label, even for input that fits none, so consider adding an "other" option. And forcing a format can lower quality if the model would have reasoned first. Let it think in free text, then constrain only the final answer.

**In today's news:** Story 5's decision models score the allowed answers directly, which is the simplest form of constrained output.

### Deep Learning: Positional Encodings

**In one sentence:** A positional encoding is extra information added to each token's vector that tells a transformer where that token sits in the sequence.

**Intuition:** Attention compares every token with every other token at once, like reading a shuffled stack of index cards. Without page numbers, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look the same. Positional encodings are the page numbers.

**Formula / Tiny example:** The original transformer uses sine waves at different frequencies: `PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d))` and `PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 10000^(2i/d))`. With vector size d = 4:
- Position 1: [sin 1, cos 1, sin 0.01, cos 0.01] = [0.8415, 0.5403, 0.0100, 1.0000]
- Position 2: [sin 2, cos 2, sin 0.02, cos 0.02] = [0.9093, −0.4161, 0.0200, 0.9998]

The first pair changes quickly with position and the second slowly, like the second and hour hands on a clock. Together they give each position a unique code. These vectors are added to the token embeddings. Many modern LLMs use **RoPE**, which rotates query and key vectors by an angle that depends on position, so attention scores depend on the *distance* between tokens.
```python
import torch
pos = torch.arange(2).unsqueeze(1) + 1           # positions 1, 2
freq = 1 / 10000 ** (torch.arange(0, 4, 2) / 4)  # [1.0, 0.01]
pe = torch.stack([torch.sin(pos*freq), torch.cos(pos*freq)], -1).flatten(1)
```

**When it's used:** Every transformer: LLMs, Vision Transformers (position of each image patch), and robot and time-series models (position in time).

**Common pitfalls:** Assuming a model works beyond the sequence length it was trained on: positions it never saw can break it, which is why context-extension methods exist. And treating position as meaning: a position signal tells the model *when*, not *what happened*, which is exactly story 3's problem.

**In today's news:** Story 3's robots indexed memory by time position, and the model learned to treat "later" as "more done."
