## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. OpenAI studied how 1,819 scientists actually use ChatGPT

- **What happened:** On Nov 16, OpenAI published "Science at the Frontier," an analysis of 580,186 science-related messages from academic scientists in its free-access program, plus a survey of 251 of them.
- **Key details:**
  - The most common single task was explaining scientific concepts (18.8% of messages). Reading and learning (27.7%) and writing (26.9%) were the biggest workflows, ahead of analysis (16.7%) and code (13.8%).
  - Asking AI to run research end to end was rare: 0.4% of the average scientist's messages, though 15.3% of scientists tried it at least once.
  - The most common difficulty was checking or correcting outputs (45.4% of survey respondents).
  - Self-reported: scientists estimated an hour of work with AI would take about 1.9 hours without it, from 1.36x for admin to 2.85x for research software.
  - OpenAI is also opening 50,000 free ChatGPT Plus grants for academic researchers on Nov 23, first come, first served; each covers the researcher plus up to two collaborators.
- **The catch:** Participants chose to apply for AI access and to answer an AI survey, so they are not typical scientists. The speed-up is people's own estimate, with no control group, and OpenAI says the findings are not causal.
- **Why it matters for you:** This is a textbook case of selection bias: a sample of enthusiastic volunteers tells you about volunteers. Also note what scientists find hardest is verifying outputs, which is exactly the skill worth practicing.
- **Source:** [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/openai-study-finds-scientists-rarely-request-end-to-end-ai-research/)

### 2. OpenAI lets four outside safety labs inside its systems

- **What happened:** On Nov 16, OpenAI announced the first phase of "embedded assessments," in which Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire examine its models and processes from the inside.
- **Key details:**
  - Access can include model checkpoints (saved copies of weights mid-training), source code, training data and monitoring systems. Sensitive work happens on-site on company devices.
  - Each group has a different job: Apollo tests whether models could secretly work against their developers; METR looks at alignment in what OpenAI calls its largest reinforcement-learning run so far; Transluce reviews OpenAI's post-training safety tests; Goodfire brings interpretability tools to monitor training.
  - Assessors can publish, but OpenAI can redact confidential details and ask for delays on risky findings. An independent board committee gets the unredacted reports.
  - Apollo and Goodfire are paid (not tied to results); METR and Transluce take no fees, though they may accept API credits or compute.
- **The catch:** It came the same day as California's lawsuit (story 6). Key pieces, including a follow-on assessment planned for early 2027, are intentions rather than commitments.
- **Why it matters for you:** "Evaluation" is turning into a job category of its own. Skills like designing tests, reading training logs and writing up limitations honestly are what these teams hire for.
- **Source:** [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/openai-signs-four-independent-assessors-for-embedded-safety-reviews/)

## Research Highlights

### 3. An audit of 40 open-LLM reports: lots of "new," little testing (arXiv:2611.20003)

- **What happened:** In a paper submitted Nov 15, Bahaeddin Türkoğlu checked what is actually new in open-weight LLMs and whether their technical reports back up their claims.
- **Key details:**
  - Of the 48 model architectures added to Hugging Face Transformers in 2025, 39 reuse at least two of their three core building blocks from an earlier model, most often Llama.
  - In 40 randomly sampled reports, only 19 of 73 claims of a new architecture or training trick came with a controlled with/without comparison.
  - Only 4 of 40 reports said they ran training more than once; 8 re-ran their competitors under the same setup; just 1 disclosed data mix, token count and compute together.
  - The paper proposes seven reporting rules for model releases.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Read the library's code to see what really changed between models, instead of trusting the report's wording.
  - For each claimed improvement, ask: did they train once with it and once without, and compare?
- **The catch:** One author, and the study doesn't judge model quality. As the paper puts it, an untested claim may still be true.
- **Why it matters for you:** When a report says "our new X boosts accuracy," look for the ablation table and for more than one run. Today's ML concept shows how to do this in your own projects.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20003)

### 4. Coding agents as coworkers: good at pieces, bad at finishing (arXiv:2611.20218)

- **What happened:** A team led by Xinping Lei and Weihao Xuan released SWE-CoWork on Nov 15, a benchmark that drops coding agents into a simulated company and asks them to deliver a feature.
- **Key details:**
  - 101 assignments from 101 real repositories, covering 11 programming languages; each simulated company has 26 colleagues with different roles and partial knowledge.
  - The agent gets a brief handoff and must ask the right people, resolve conflicting information and then code. Hidden tests grade the result.
  - Across 11 models, the top two passed about 63% of the target tests but completed fewer than 8% of whole assignments.
  - Claude Opus 5.5 usually asked the right owner first (93% of first questions) but still gathered only 64% of the requirements; 31% of requirements that agents reported as done failed their tests.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Most coding benchmarks hand the agent a complete task. Real jobs start with an unclear ticket.
  - Measuring "passed some tests" separately from "finished the assignment" shows the gap between partial and complete work.
- **The catch:** The colleagues are simulated by models, so real coworkers could be more or less helpful.
- **Why it matters for you:** Asking clarifying questions and tracking requirements to the end are skills that separate good data scientists too. And "the agent said it's done" is not evidence; run the tests.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20218)

## Tools & Libraries

### 5. MLPerf Training adds its first post-training benchmark

- **What happened:** On Nov 16, MLCommons published MLPerf Training v6.1, the industry's standard speed test for training, with 250 results from 24 organizations, its largest round so far.
- **Key details:**
  - New test: reinforcement learning (GRPO, today's LLM concept) on Qwen3.5-397B, a mixture-of-experts model with 17B active parameters. The model fixes code in a Linux container, earns a pass/fail reward from hidden tests, and samples 16 answers per prompt. It drew 29 submissions.
  - A new recommender test, DLRMv4-HSTU, predicts user engagement from the order and timing of past interactions.
  - Fastest GRPO times (vendor-reported): Nvidia Vera Rubin NVL72 at 10.44 minutes, Google's Ironwood TPUs at 18.02 minutes on 640 chips, CoreWeave GB200 at 22.78 minutes on 256 GPUs.
  - Like-for-like progress: the best FLUX.1 image-model time on 512 accelerators fell 2.49x in six months, from 17.12 to 6.88 minutes. 72% of results came from multi-node systems.
- **Background:** "Post-training" is the stage after pretraining where a model is tuned (for example with reinforcement learning) to follow instructions or solve tasks.
- **The catch:** The GRPO times aren't directly comparable: systems used different chips, chip counts and splits between generating answers and training. MLCommons itself doesn't name winners.
- **Why it matters for you:** A benchmark number only means something alongside its setup. Fixing everything except one variable is the same discipline as today's ablation concept.
- **Source:** [TechArena](https://techarena.ai/content/in-record-round-mlperf-training-6-1-reaches-into-post-training) · [Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/330890/20261117/mlperf-training-v61-adds-post-training-test-nvidia-google-coreweave-results-not-comparable.htm)

**Also noted:** Salesforce released [SOV-Transcribe-1](https://huggingface.co/blog/salesforce-sovereign-intelligence/sov-transcribe-1) (Nov 12), an Apache 2.0 speech-to-text model with about 4B active parameters; Salesforce reports a 2.28% word error rate on Artificial Analysis's leaderboard, the best among open-weight models.

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Ablation Studies

**In one sentence:** An ablation study measures how much each part of a model or pipeline matters by removing it, retraining, and comparing results with everything else held fixed.

**Intuition:** A baker wondering if the expensive vanilla matters bakes two batches that are identical except for the vanilla. If tasters can't tell, the vanilla isn't doing much.

**Formula / Tiny example:** You add a new feature block and train with 3 random seeds each. With it: 0.861, 0.855, 0.858 accuracy (mean 0.858). Without it: 0.852, 0.860, 0.849 (mean 0.854). The gap is about 0.4 points, smaller than the spread within each group (0.849 to 0.860), so the evidence is weak. If you had run only seed 3 (0.858 vs 0.849), you'd have wrongly claimed a 0.9-point gain.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
for drop in [None, "income", "age"]:          # one row per ablation
    cols = [c for c in X.columns if c != drop]
    s = cross_val_score(RandomForestClassifier(random_state=0), X[cols], y, cv=5)
    print(drop, round(s.mean(), 3), "+/-", round(s.std(), 3))
```

**When it's used:** Research papers (the "ablation table"), feature selection, and checking whether a complicated pipeline step is worth its cost.

**Common pitfall(s):** Changing two things at once, so you can't tell which helped. Using a single run, so noise looks like an effect.

**In today's news:** Story 3 found only 19 of 73 novelty claims in open-LLM reports had this kind of with/without test, and only 4 of 40 reports mentioned more than one run.

### LLMs: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

**In one sentence:** GRPO is a reinforcement-learning method that samples several answers to the same prompt and pushes the model toward the answers that scored better than their group's average.

**Intuition:** A teacher grades a group project by having each student solve the same problem, then praising whoever beat the class average and nudging the rest. No separate judge is needed to say what a "normal" score is; the group sets the bar.

**Formula / Tiny example:** For each answer, advantage = (reward − group mean) / group standard deviation. Four answers score [1, 0, 0, 1]: mean 0.5, std 0.5, so advantages are [+1, −1, −1, +1]. If only one succeeds, [1, 0, 0, 0]: mean 0.25, std ≈ 0.433, so the winner gets +1.73 and each loser −0.58. Rare successes get a bigger push.

```python
import numpy as np
r = np.array([1, 0, 0, 0], dtype=float)     # rewards for 4 samples
adv = (r - r.mean()) / (r.std() + 1e-8)     # group-relative advantage
print(adv.round(2))                         # [ 1.73 -0.58 -0.58 -0.58]
```

**When it's used:** Training reasoning and coding models with automatically checkable rewards (unit tests, math answers). It skips the separate "value" network that older methods like PPO use, saving memory.

**Common pitfall(s):** If every sample in a group gets the same reward (all pass or all fail), the std is 0 and the advantages are all 0, so that prompt teaches nothing; prompts need to be neither too easy nor too hard. Small groups make the average noisy.

**In today's news:** Story 5's new MLPerf test is a GRPO run on Qwen3.5-397B with 16 answers sampled per prompt and pass/fail rewards from hidden tests.

### Deep Learning: Data Parallelism (Training on Many GPUs)

**In one sentence:** Data parallelism trains one model on many GPUs at once by giving each GPU a full copy of the model and a different slice of each batch, then averaging their gradients before every update.

**Intuition:** Four people grading one big stack of exams: each takes a quarter, writes down their average score, and they combine the four averages into one class average. Same answer as one person grading everything, four times faster.

**Formula / Tiny example:** 8 GPUs × 32 examples each = a global batch of 256. Suppose 4 GPUs compute gradients 0.2, 0.4, −0.1 and 0.3 for some weight on equal-sized slices. The "all-reduce" step averages them: (0.2 + 0.4 − 0.1 + 0.3) / 4 = 0.8 / 4 = 0.2. Every GPU applies that same 0.2, so all copies stay identical.

```python
# launch with: torchrun --nproc_per_node=4 train.py
import torch.distributed as dist
from torch.nn.parallel import DistributedDataParallel as DDP
dist.init_process_group("nccl")
model = DDP(model.to(local_rank), device_ids=[local_rank])
sampler = torch.utils.data.DistributedSampler(dataset)   # each GPU gets a different slice
# the training loop is unchanged; DDP averages gradients during loss.backward()
```

**When it's used:** Almost every large training run, often combined with splitting the model itself across GPUs when it's too big for one.

**Common pitfall(s):** Forgetting that the global batch grows with GPU count, so the learning rate may need retuning. Forgetting the distributed sampler (or `sampler.set_epoch(epoch)`), so GPUs see duplicate data or the same order every epoch.

**In today's news:** In story 5, 72% of MLPerf results came from multi-node systems, and the largest run used 10,240 accelerators.
