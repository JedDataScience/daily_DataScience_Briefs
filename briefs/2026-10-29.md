## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. Google's Gemini 4 Carbon tops a new leaderboard, but few people can use it yet

- **What happened:** On Oct 28, Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon, a frontier model aimed at cybersecurity and long, multi-step tasks, two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon.
- **Key details:**
  - **Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5:** Carbon 59, Claude Opus 5.5 58, GPT-6 Astra 57, Argon 50 (see story 4 for what changed in this index).
  - **Cybersecurity (self-reported):** 88.2% on SEC-bench Pro (finding real vulnerabilities), 54.8% on ExploitGym (up from Argon's 42.0%) and 71.0% on CWE-bench (writing working fixes).
  - **Not a clean sweep:** On FrontierSWE 2 (coding) Carbon scored 61.2% vs Astra's 65.5% and Opus's 62.3%. On the OSWorld offline subset (computer use) it scored 81.5% vs Opus's 87.2%.
  - **Access and price:** It goes first to selected cyber defenders in Google's Fairwind program, with no public API yet. Google listed introductory pricing of $2/$10 per million input/output tokens, rising later to $4/$20.
- **Background:** A **long-horizon task** needs many steps in a row, like fixing a bug across a codebase. An error early on can spoil everything after it, so these tasks are much harder than single questions.
- **The catch:** On Artificial Analysis's knowledge test (AA-Omniscience), Carbon answered more questions correctly than Argon (58% vs 48%), but its hallucination rate rose from 4% to 11%. Google's cyber evaluations were run "without guardrails," it hasn't published a technical report, and some leads are within a point.
- **Why it matters for you:** A model can get smarter on average and still make things up more often. Read both numbers, not just the headline score (see today's LLM concept).

### 2. NVIDIA releases Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe, an open speech model for 40 languages

- **What happened:** On Oct 28, NVIDIA released Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe, an open-weight model for transcription, translation and speaker labeling. It also released an expanded speech dataset, Granary v2.
- **Key details:**
  - **Size:** About 34B total parameters, with about 4.3B active per token, per NVIDIA's technical report. It handles recordings up to one hour long.
  - **What it does:** Transcribes 40 languages, translates 31 to or from English, and labels up to eight speakers (in 13 languages). One checkpoint switches tasks depending on the prompt, and you can supply jargon in the prompt to improve accuracy.
  - **Accuracy (self-reported):** 4.71% word error rate (WER) on the English Open ASR leaderboard, the best among open-weight models. The next-best open model in NVIDIA's comparison, Qwen3-ASR-1.7B, scored 4.94%.
  - **License:** OpenMDW-1.1, which allows commercial use. Weights are on Hugging Face.
- **Background:** **Word error rate** counts the words a system substitutes, deletes or inserts, divided by the number of words in the correct transcript. Lower is better, and 4.71% means about 1 mistake per 21 words.
- **The catch:** It works on recordings after the fact, not live audio. Audio question-answering was only tested in English. Coverage gives different total hours for Granary v2, so check the dataset card before quoting a figure.
- **Why it matters for you:** Interviews, lectures and customer calls are easy to turn into text data with a model like this. Remember to measure WER on your own audio, because accents and domain jargon can make it much worse than on a leaderboard.
- **Sources:** [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/nvidia-launches-open-weight-speech-model-and-463-062-hour-audio-dataset/) · [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/28/nvidia-releases-all-in-one-adaptable-nemotron-3-5-audio-transcription-and-translation-model/) · [NVIDIA technical report (PDF)](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/nemotron/files/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Transcribe-Technical-Report.pdf)

## Research Highlights

### 3. Analysts adopted their AI agent's hidden preferences 86% of the time

- **What it is:** A study by Amaç Herdağdelen, Hailey Joren and Alex Tamkin of Anthropic's Alignment team, published Oct 27. It looked at how AI agents shape experts' judgment calls in data analysis.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - 61 professional analysts experienced in causal inference were asked to advise a fictional farming co-op on whether to expand a paid advisory program. 50 of them used a coding agent (Claude Opus 4.8 in Claude Code).
  - The task had four choices where both options were defensible: the outcome measure, the comparison method, which farms to include, and how to weight them.
  - For each choice, a hidden coin flip gave the agent a "lean" toward one option. The agent was told to argue that lean honestly and change its mind only if the evidence demanded it.
- **Results:**
  - Analysts matched the agent's lean 86% of the time, where chance would be about 50%.
  - When an analyst started with a preference and the agent disagreed, they switched 60% of the time, compared with 6% when the agent agreed.
  - 77% repeated the agent's arguments in their write-ups, but only 15% credited the agent. Experience and education didn't predict who deferred.
  - Separately, 6 of 11 analysts working without AI made serious methodological errors, vs 3 of 50 with AI. The authors note those groups weren't directly comparable.
- **The catch:** The leans were deliberately made stronger than usual. The study covered one task, one field and one model.
- **Why it matters for you:** Many choices in an analysis have no single right answer, and an assistant's default can quietly become yours. Before you ask the AI, write down your own choice for each key decision and your reason, then compare.
- **Source:** [Anthropic Research](https://anthropic.com/research/agents-judgment-calls)

### 4. Artificial Analysis rebuilds its AI leaderboard around private tests

- **What happened:** On Oct 28, Artificial Analysis released version 5 of its Intelligence Index, a composite score built from many benchmarks and one of the most-cited model leaderboards.
- **Key details:**
  - **60% private:** For the first time, most of the index uses private test sets or held-out answers. The stated reason is contamination: public test questions end up in web-scraped training data.
  - **New tests:** FrontierCode 2.0 (Cognition's 132 private coding tasks written by open-source maintainers) and Terminal-Bench-Science (70 research tasks in a terminal). Humanity's Last Exam was replaced by a cleaned-up 1,000-question "HLE-Diamond."
  - **Scores before Carbon:** Claude Opus 5.5 58, GPT-6 Astra 57, GPT-6.1 Sol 55, Gemini 4 Argon 50. Scores are lower overall than in v4 and can't be compared across versions.
  - **Open models fell behind:** The gap between the best open-weight model and the leader widened from 3 points to 12. Kimi K3.1 Pro scores 46 but its weights aren't out yet. Among models you can download today, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM-5.3 lead at 32.
- **Background:** **Contamination** is a form of data leakage. If test questions were in the training data, a high score may reflect memorization rather than skill.
- **The catch:** Private tests are harder to game, but outsiders can't inspect them either, so you have to trust the evaluator.
- **Why it matters for you:** It's the same rule as keeping a test set untouched in your own projects. A score only means something if the model never saw those examples.
- **Sources:** [OfficeChai](https://officechai.com/ai/artificial-analysis-launches-intelligence-index-v5-with-majority-private-datasets-openai-models-gain-muse-wonder-kimi-drop/) · [TechApple](https://global.techapple.com/2026/10/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index-v5/)

## Tools & Libraries

### 5. Polars 2.1: huge group-bys now spill to disk, plus recursive SQL

- **What happened:** On Oct 27, the Polars team released Polars 2.1, three weeks after 2.0 made the streaming engine the default.
- **Key details:**
  - **Out-of-core group-by:** Group-bys with very many distinct keys can now spill to disk instead of running out of memory. In the team's example, a group-by over 30 million keys finished with a 200 MB memory budget. Window functions (`over`) can spill too.
  - **SQL:** Adds `WITH RECURSIVE` queries (useful for hierarchies such as org charts), temp tables, `ASOF JOIN`, `generate_series` and `DATE_TRUNC`.
  - **Data lake formats:** You can attach Iceberg catalogs to `SQLContext`, read and write encrypted Parquet, and try experimental GeoParquet. These features are marked unstable.
  - **Breaking changes:** Python 3.10 is no longer supported. Adding a string to a number in eager mode now raises an error, where `pl.Series([1, 2]) + "4"` used to return `["14", "24"]`.
- **Background:** **Out-of-core** means processing data larger than your RAM by working in chunks and temporarily writing pieces to disk.
- **Why it matters for you:** A laptop can now do group-bys that used to need a cluster. The string-arithmetic change is a good example of a library catching a silent bug for you. Run `pip install -U polars` and re-run your tests.
- **Source:** [Polars blog](https://pola.rs/posts/polars-2-1-changelog/)

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Class Imbalance

**In one sentence:** Class imbalance means one class is much rarer than the others in your data, like fraud, disease or equipment failure.

**Intuition:** A smoke detector that never beeps is "right" on almost every day of the year, and useless on the one day there's a fire. A model trained on imbalanced data drifts toward that lazy strategy unless you stop it.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Say you have 1,000 transactions and 20 are fraud (2%). A model that always predicts "not fraud" scores 98% accuracy and 0% recall. A common fix is to weight each class by `n_samples / (n_classes × n_in_class)`:
- Fraud: 1000 / (2 × 20) = **25**
- Not fraud: 1000 / (2 × 980) ≈ **0.51**

So each fraud example counts about 49 times as much as a normal one during training.

```python
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, stratify=y, test_size=0.2)
clf = LogisticRegression(class_weight="balanced").fit(X_tr, y_tr)
```

**When it's used:** Fraud detection, medical screening, churn prediction, defect detection and rare-event forecasting.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Judging the model by accuracy. Use precision, recall or PR-AUC instead, and tune the decision threshold.
- Oversampling (for example, with SMOTE) *before* the train/test split. Copies of the same examples end up in both sets, which is leakage. Resample only the training data, inside each cross-validation fold.

### LLMs: Hallucination

**In one sentence:** A hallucination is when a language model states something false or made-up with the same confidence it uses for facts.

**Intuition:** Picture a student on a multiple-choice exam with no penalty for wrong answers. Guessing always beats leaving a blank. LLMs are trained and often graded the same way, so they learn to produce a plausible answer instead of saying "I don't know."

**Formula / Tiny example:** Two models each answer 100 factual questions (made-up numbers for illustration):

| | Correct | Wrong | "I don't know" | Accuracy | Score (correct − wrong) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Model A | 58 | 11 | 31 | 58% | **47** |
| Model B | 62 | 38 | 0 | 62% | **24** |

Model B wins on accuracy, but it's wrong 38 times, so you can't trust its answers. Penalizing wrong answers makes Model A the better model. This is why evaluations like AA-Omniscience report a hallucination rate alongside accuracy.

**When it's used:** Hallucination checks are part of evaluating any LLM system. Common ways to reduce it are retrieval (RAG), asking the model to cite sources, checking answers against a database, and letting it say "I don't know."

**Common pitfalls:**
- Assuming a newer or "smarter" model hallucinates less. It can be the reverse.
- Trusting citations without opening them, because models can invent references that look real.

**In today's news:** Gemini 4 Carbon answered more questions correctly than Argon, but its hallucination rate went from 4% to 11% (story 1).

### Deep Learning: Activation Functions

**In one sentence:** An activation function is the non-linear "bend" applied to each neuron's output, which lets a network learn more than straight-line relationships.

**Intuition:** Stacking linear layers is like stacking pieces of glass: however many you add, you can only look straight through. Activations are what let a network bend its input into curves and corners. Without them, a 100-layer network can only do what a single linear layer can.

**Formula / Tiny example:** For the inputs `[-2, 0, 3]`:
- **ReLU**, `max(0, x)`: gives `[0, 0, 3]`.
- **Sigmoid**, `1 / (1 + e^(-x))`: gives `[0.119, 0.5, 0.953]`.
- **GELU**, `x · Φ(x)` (used in most transformers; Φ is the standard normal CDF): gives `[-0.046, 0, 2.996]`.

Why sigmoid fell out of favor for hidden layers: its slope is never larger than 0.25. Backpropagation multiplies slopes across layers, so 10 sigmoid layers can shrink a gradient by 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 0.00000095. This is the **vanishing gradient** problem. ReLU's slope is exactly 1 for positive inputs, which avoids it.

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn
x = torch.tensor([-2., 0., 3.])
print(nn.ReLU()(x), torch.sigmoid(x), nn.GELU()(x))
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(10, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 1))
```

**When it's used:** ReLU in CNNs and MLPs, GELU and SwiGLU in transformers, sigmoid on binary-classification outputs and softmax on multi-class outputs.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Putting an activation on the final layer of a regression model, which clips the outputs (for example, ReLU can never predict a negative number).
- "Dead ReLUs": if a neuron's input is always negative, its gradient is always zero and it stops learning. A high learning rate makes this more likely. Leaky ReLU or GELU help.
