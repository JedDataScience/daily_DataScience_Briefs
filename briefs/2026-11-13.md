## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. Google's Gemini 4 Hydrogen: a cheaper Gemini 4 with adjustable "thinking"

- **What happened:** On Nov 12, Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a fast, lower-cost model adapted from its larger Gemini 4 Carbon. It is the recommended upgrade from Gemini 3.8 Flash.
- **Key details:**
  - Price: $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens. That is double Gemini 3.8 Flash's current price ($0.75/$3.75), but Flash rises to the same price on Jan 1, 2027.
  - 1-million-token context window (shared by input, thinking and output), and four thinking levels: low, medium, high and xhigh. Google warns that xhigh can "overthink" simple prompts.
  - Independent Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index: 51 at default settings (54 at xhigh), vs 31 for 3.8 Flash and 55 for Claude Sonnet 5.5.
  - A new Antigravity "Orchestrator" mode has Carbon plan and Hydrogen do the work. Google says it gets near-Carbon results on Terminal-Bench with 24% less quota and 19% faster (self-reported).
- **Background:** A "thinking level" sets how many hidden reasoning tokens the model may spend before answering. More thinking usually means better answers on hard problems, but higher cost and slower replies.
- **The catch:** Most head-to-head benchmarks come from Google's own table. Its model card also reports unjustified refusals rose 5.57 percentage points vs 3.8 Flash, and Hydrogen outputs text only.
- **Why it matters for you:** "Plan with a big model, execute with a small one" is now a product feature, not just a research idea. When you build with LLM APIs, compare cost per finished task, not just price per token.
- **Source:** [Fello AI](https://felloai.com/gemini-4-hydrogen/) · [AI Understanding](https://aiunderstanding.org/news/google-unveils-gemini-4-hydrogen-with-new-pricing-and-multi-model-orchestrator)

### 2. Thinking Machines opens Inkling 2 Small's weights under Apache 2.0

- **What happened:** On Nov 12, Thinking Machines Lab posted the weights for Inkling 2 Small on Hugging Face. It launched on Nov 5 as hosted-only, and we reported then that open weights were promised after safety testing.
- **Key details:**
  - 594B total parameters, 17B active per token (about 2.9%). It is a mixture-of-experts model that routes each token to 8 of 384 experts.
  - Takes text and images, with up to 1M tokens of context.
  - Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index: 31, up from 13 for the first Inkling Small. It is weak on terminal coding (13% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 vs 30% for Claude Sonnet 5.5).
  - Self-hosting is a cluster job: about 500 GB of combined GPU memory for the 4-bit (NVFP4) checkpoint, or about 800 GB for 8-bit. Hosted use on Tinker costs $0.30/$1.00 per million input/output tokens.
- **Background:** At 4 bits (half a byte) per weight, 594B parameters is about 297 GB of raw weights. Real memory needs are higher because the model also needs room for activations and the KV cache.
- **The catch:** The lab's own report says the model trails closed systems on alignment tests, and outside red-team results haven't been published.
- **Why it matters for you:** Apache 2.0 means you can use, modify and sell it with few strings attached. The hardware bill is now the barrier, not the license, so "open" doesn't automatically mean you can run it on your laptop.
- **Source:** [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai/news/thinking-machines-opens-inkling-2-small-s-594b-weights-to-developers) · [DEV Community](https://dev.to/paullovelace/inkling-2-small-weights-are-open-download-fine-tune-self-host-3l8b)

## Research Highlights

### 3. Do LLM labelers follow your definition? Only partly (arXiv:2611.13339)

- **What happened:** A paper posted Nov 11 by researchers including Bogdan Mamaev and Christopher Leckie tests whether LLMs used as data annotators really apply the definition in the prompt, or fall back on what they learned in pretraining.
- **Key details:**
  - The authors labeled the same texts under two *competing* definitions of a concept, and under *paraphrased* versions of one definition as a control.
  - Across six social-science concepts and three LLMs, swapping the definition always changed more labels, on average, than paraphrasing it. So models do respond to definitions.
  - The size of that effect varies by concept and model, so the definition you write can quietly shape your dataset.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - If a model is really reading your definition, changing *what* it says should move labels much more than changing *how* it's worded.
  - The paraphrase control tells you how much labels jump around for no real reason. The definition swap tells you how much the definition matters.
  - The authors recommend reporting this "definition sensitivity" alongside any LLM-labeled dataset.
- **Why it matters for you:** Using an LLM to label data is now routine in class projects and jobs. Before you trust the labels, rerun on a sample with a reworded prompt and with an alternative definition, and report how much the labels change.
- **Source:** [arXiv: cs.CL new submissions, Nov 11](https://arxiv.org/list/cs.CL/new)

### 4. The "token tax": Indian languages can cost up to 5x more to process (arXiv:2611.13058)

- **What happened:** A paper posted Nov 11 by Johin Johny Arimbur, Ankur Ashit Ghosh and Shervin Sam measures how many more tokens Indian languages need than English across nine public tokenizers.
- **Key details:**
  - Using translated parallel text (FLORES-200), GPT-4o's tokenizer needs 1.57x the English token count for Hindi and 5.01x for Odia to say the same thing.
  - Because APIs bill per token, the same request can cost several times more, and a fixed context window holds less text.
  - A separate paper the same day (arXiv:2611.13441) found premiums of up to 10x for Amharic in older tokenizers and 6.8x in the newest commercial one.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Tokenizers learn their vocabulary mostly from English-heavy data, so English words often become one token while other scripts get split into many small pieces.
  - Take the same sentences in two languages, count tokens in each, and divide. That ratio is the "tax."
  - The cost figures are the authors' estimates from token counts, not measured bills.
- **Why it matters for you:** If your project handles multilingual text, measure token counts per language before you budget API costs or pick a context length. It's a five-line script with any tokenizer library.
- **Source:** [arXiv: cs.CL new submissions, Nov 11](https://arxiv.org/list/cs.CL/new)

## Tools & Libraries

### 5. Huawei open-sources MACaQ, a fast 4-bit quantizer for many model types

- **What happened:** Huawei's Computing Systems Lab released MACaQ on GitHub under Apache 2.0, with a paper (arXiv:2611.10649, submitted Nov 7) describing the method. It drew attention this week.
- **Key details:**
  - Quantizes both weights and activations to 4 bits (W4A4) and exports 2- to 4-bit GGUF files for llama.cpp, plus vLLM, Diffusers (image/video/audio) and PyTorch speech models.
  - Claims a 122B-parameter model was quantized in 64 minutes using 32.5 GB of GPU memory.
  - Llama-3-8B perplexity on WikiText-2: 6.90 with MACaQ vs 7.13 for MR-GPTQ, 7.48 for DuQuant++ and 6.14 at full 16-bit precision (lower is better; self-reported).
  - On Qwen3.8-27B in vLLM, perplexity goes from 2.8463 (BF16) to 2.8994 (about 1.9% worse) while prefill runs about 2.3x and decode about 2.6x faster (self-reported).
- **Background:** Each layer rescales its input channels and applies a fast "Hadamard rotation" so a few huge activation values don't wreck the 4-bit rounding. Because each layer is handled on its own, the tool works on new architectures without custom code. The rescaling is a close cousin to today's ML concept, feature scaling.
- **The catch:** The repo is new (16 commits, 35 stars), installation is per-backend with no single pip package, and all numbers so far are the authors' own.
- **Why it matters for you:** Quantization is how big open models end up running on student hardware. MACaQ reports results in perplexity, so today's LLM concept will help you read tables like this one.
- **Source:** [GitHub: huawei-csl/MACaQ](https://github.com/huawei-csl/MACaQ) · [arXiv:2611.10649](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.10649)

**Also noted:** Zyphra released [ZONOS3-Mini](https://www.llmreference.com/model/zonos3-mini), an Apache 2.0 text-to-speech model with 800M total and 100M active parameters that it says runs in real time on phones and in the browser.

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Feature Scaling (Standardization)

**In one sentence:** Feature scaling puts numeric features on comparable ranges, most often by standardizing each one to mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

**Intuition:** Comparing a runner's time in seconds with their weight in grams makes weight look enormous just because of its units. Scaling converts both to "how unusual is this value for this feature," so neither dominates by accident.

**Formula / Tiny example:** z = (x − mean) / std. Heights 150, 170, 190 cm have mean 170 and std 16.33, so they become −1.22, 0, 1.22. Incomes of $30k, $50k, $70k become the same −1.22, 0, 1.22, even though they started 200x larger.

```python
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier())
model.fit(X_train, y_train)   # scaler learns mean/std from training data only
```

**When it's used:** Distance-based models (k-NN, k-means, SVMs), PCA, and anything trained by gradient descent, including regularized logistic regression and neural networks. Tree models (random forests, gradient boosting) generally don't need it.

**Common pitfall(s):** Fitting the scaler on the full dataset before splitting leaks test-set information into training. Put the scaler inside a pipeline so it is fit on training folds only. Also, standardization is sensitive to outliers; `RobustScaler` (median and IQR) is safer when a few values are extreme.

**In today's news:** MACaQ (story 5) rescales each layer's input channels before rounding to 4 bits, the same idea of taming very different value ranges.

### LLMs: Perplexity

**In one sentence:** Perplexity measures how surprised a language model is by real text: lower means it predicted the actual next tokens with higher probability.

**Intuition:** A perplexity of 4 means the model is, on average, as unsure as if it were choosing fairly among 4 equally likely next tokens. A perfect model that always knew the next token would score 1.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Perplexity = exp(average negative log-probability of the true tokens). Say a model gave the true next tokens probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.5 and 0.125. In bits that's 1, 2, 1 and 3, averaging 1.75 bits, so perplexity = 2^1.75 ≈ 3.36.

```python
import math
probs = [0.5, 0.25, 0.5, 0.125]
ppl = math.exp(-sum(math.log(p) for p in probs) / len(probs))  # 3.36
```

**When it's used:** Tracking pretraining progress, comparing quantized models with their originals, and checking whether a fine-tune damaged general language ability.

**Common pitfall(s):** Perplexities are only comparable with the same tokenizer and the same test text. A tokenizer that splits text into more, easier pieces changes the score without the model getting better. Also, low perplexity doesn't guarantee good answers to instructions or correct facts.

**In today's news:** MACaQ (story 5) reports 6.90 vs 6.14 at full precision on Llama-3-8B, and the token-tax paper (story 4) is a reminder of why tokenizers make cross-model comparisons tricky.

### Deep Learning: Early Stopping

**In one sentence:** Early stopping ends training when performance on a validation set stops improving, and keeps the weights from the best epoch.

**Intuition:** It's like studying for an exam with practice tests: once your practice scores start dropping because you've memorized the answer key instead of learning, you stop and go with your best version.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Validation loss by epoch: 0.90, 0.70, 0.62, **0.60**, 0.61, 0.63. With "patience" = 2 (allow two epochs without improvement), training stops after epoch 6 and restores the epoch-4 weights.

```python
best, bad, patience = float("inf"), 0, 2
for epoch in range(100):
    train_one_epoch(model)
    val = evaluate(model, val_loader)
    if val < best:
        best, bad = val, 0
        torch.save(model.state_dict(), "best.pt")
    else:
        bad += 1
        if bad >= patience:
            break
model.load_state_dict(torch.load("best.pt"))
```

**When it's used:** Almost any neural network training run, and gradient-boosting libraries (XGBoost, LightGBM `early_stopping_rounds`). It acts as cheap regularization and saves compute.

**Common pitfall(s):** Using the test set to decide when to stop, which makes your reported test score optimistic; stop on a separate validation set. Setting patience too low on a noisy validation curve can stop training before it has really plateaued.
