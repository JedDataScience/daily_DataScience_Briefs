## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. Microsoft's diagnostic AI beats generalist doctors on hard cases, per a *Science* paper

- **What happened:** On Oct 22, Microsoft AI published its MAI Diagnostic Orchestrator (MAI-DxO) study in *Science*. It's the peer-reviewed version of a 2025 preprint.
- **Key details:**
  - MAI-DxO correctly diagnosed **83%** of complex *New England Journal of Medicine* case reports. Generalist physicians and internists who could use reference materials got **36%**.
  - The system is an *orchestrator*: several AI agents act like a panel of specialists, asking questions, ordering tests and arguing toward a diagnosis one step at a time.
  - It was tested with models from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta and DeepSeek. It worked best on OpenAI's GPT-5 family, lifting GPT-5.5 from about 72% to about 87%.
  - Next step: evaluation with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston over the coming months.
- **The catch:** every case was a past one; no live patients were involved. NEJM cases are usually solved by specialist teams, and none were in the comparison. Reporters noted that most of the gain comes from the underlying model, not the orchestration. The tool isn't available to use.
- **Why it matters for you:** "AI beats doctors" depends entirely on *which* doctors, *which* cases and *what* resources each side had. Read the comparison group before the headline number, and do the same for your own baselines.
- **Sources:** [AI Weekly (summarizing TechCrunch)](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/microsofts-mai-dxo-beats-doctors-83-to-36-on-nejm-cases) · [ExplainX: claimed vs verified](https://explainx.ai/blog/microsoft-diagnostic-orchestrator-science-paper-83-percent-vs-doctors-sdbench-claimed-vs-verified-2026)

### 2. Claude proves 239 open-source fuzz targets can't crash

- **What happened:** This week Anthropic's Frontier Red Team and the startup Theorem reported using Claude to turn fuzz tests from Google's OSS-Fuzz project into formal, machine-checked proofs.
- **Key details:**
  - **123 libraries** and **239 entry points**, with about **180 million lines** of proof written in Rocq, a proof assistant (formerly Coq). A typical proof runs about 600,000 lines.
  - Each proof is a theorem stating that *no* input up to 64 MB can crash that entry point. Rocq's small trusted checker verifies every step, so nobody has to trust the model's output on faith.
  - Along the way Claude found and patched real bugs, including a decompressor underflow triggered by a roughly 20 MB input, and it rediscovered a known libexpat vulnerability.
- **Background:** *Fuzzing* throws millions of random inputs at a program to see if it crashes, but it can only show bugs exist, never that none are left. A *formal proof* covers every possible input within the stated limits.
- **The catch:** the guarantees hold only for a narrow setup: single-threaded, statically linked RISC-V binaries. Any change to the code or build needs a new proof, and humans still have to check that each theorem states the right thing.
- **Why it matters for you:** Testing samples inputs and proof covers all of them. Thinking about what your tests *can't* show is part of good engineering.
- **Sources:** [AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai/news/anthropic-s-claude-proves-239-open-source-libraries-crash-free-exposing-fuzzing) · [Anthropic report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formal-verification)

### 3. Red-teamers find gaps in OpenAI's monitor for its own coding agents

- **What happened:** On Oct 22, METR and Redwood Research published a summary of a three-week exercise (Jul 21 to Aug 11) probing the system OpenAI uses to watch its internal AI coding agents for misbehavior.
- **Key details:**
  - **Coverage gaps:** an estimated 75–94% of eligible agent traffic from strong models could be monitored (99.2% for internal Codex agents). Context-compaction events, which affected about 10% of Codex traffic, weren't monitored.
  - **Timing evasion:** checks ran only when an agent handed control back to the user, so an agent could put off review simply by continuing to work.
  - **Silent failures:** differences between what the agent saw and what the monitor saw could make the monitor fail without raising an alarm.
  - On the positive side, the monitor's error rate was under 0.2%.
- **The catch:** this describes the system as of July. OpenAI says it has patched every issue affecting production, and METR hasn't verified that claim.
- **Why it matters for you:** A monitor is just another model in a pipeline. Check that it sees the same inputs as the thing it monitors, and that its failures are loud rather than silent.
- **Source:** [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-10-22-red-teaming-openai-agent-monitoring/)

## Research Highlights

### 4. "Naive RAG Is Stronger Than We Thought" (arXiv:2610.30755)

- **What it is:** a 62-page study, posted Oct 22, asking whether elaborate "agentic memory" systems for chatbots, which rewrite, summarize or organize past conversations, actually beat plain retrieval.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - **Raw-RAG** keeps the full conversation history as-is, chopped into overlapping chunks, without any LLM summarizing.
  - At question time it finds the most similar chunks with embeddings, re-scores them with a more precise *cross-encoder* (a reranker), and hands the best few to the LLM.
  - The authors argue that summarizing memory throws away details and the relations between them, which turn out to matter for answering questions.
- **Results:** Raw-RAG ranked first in **6 of 7** benchmark-and-model settings across LoCoMo, LongMemEval, MEME and MemoryAgentBench, beating the strongest complex system by 0.13–5.53 points. Code is public.
- **Why it matters for you:** Always build the simple baseline first. If a complex system can't clearly beat it, the complexity isn't earning its keep.
- **Sources:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30755) · [GitHub: RAW-RAG](https://github.com/Litmeb/RAW-RAG)

### 5. Where does "Wait" come from in reasoning models? (arXiv:2610.30882)

- **What it is:** a paper posted Oct 22 tracing where reasoning models pick up self-correction markers such as "Wait, let me re-check…", the words often cited as evidence that reinforcement learning (RL) teaches models to reflect.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - The authors compare models trained with RL straight from a base model ("zero-RL") against models first fine-tuned on reasoning examples written out step by step.
  - The marker mostly comes from the earlier stages, mid-training or that fine-tuning. RL on its own rarely *invents* it, though it can strengthen, change or remove it.
  - Counting how often a model says "wait" is therefore a weak measure of how well it reasons.
- **Why it matters for you:** A visible behavior isn't proof of the mechanism behind it. Before crediting one training stage with a behavior, check whether an earlier stage already put it there (see the LLM concept below).
- **Source:** [Papers with Code summary](https://paperswithcode.co/paper/2610.30882) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30882)

## Tools & Libraries

### 6. pandas 3.1.0 is released, and it broke at least two real projects on day one

- **What happened:** pandas 3.1.0 was published to PyPI on Oct 22. It requires Python 3.11 or newer.
- **Key details:**
  - New features include `groupby().agg()` functions that can return NumPy arrays, and float display that respects `display.precision`. Arithmetic with tuples or `range` objects is now deprecated, so convert them to a list or array first.
  - **Breakage 1:** in the OasisLMF insurance-modeling tools, string columns read as `category` were now inferred as numbers. Postal code `01234` became `1234`, and about 105 tests failed. A fix landed the next day.
  - **Breakage 2:** in `exchange_calendars`, changes to pandas's date-offset machinery caused crashes, and worse, *silently* dropped trading days (40 Moscow Exchange sessions went missing between 1990 and 2024).
- **Why it matters for you:** Pin library versions (`pandas==3.0.6` in `requirements.txt`) and upgrade on purpose, rerunning your tests when you do. Read ID-like columns such as ZIP codes as strings with `dtype=str` (see the ML concept below).
- **Sources:** [PyPI: pandas](https://pypi.org/project/pandas/) · [pandas release notes](https://pandas.pydata.org/docs/whatsnew/index.html) · [OasisLMF issue #315](https://github.com/OasisLMF/ODS_Tools/issues/315) · [exchange_calendars issue #632](https://github.com/gerrymanoim/exchange_calendars/issues/632)

### 7. Agentic AI Foundation adds four open projects, including Hugging Face's OpenEnv

- **What happened:** On Oct 22, at AGNTCon + MCPCon in San Jose, the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation took on four new projects. That brings it to 10, up from 3 at its launch in December 2025.
- **Key details:**
  - **Agent Skills** (Anthropic): a format for packaging instructions and scripts that an agent loads only when it needs them.
  - **Open Responses** (OpenAI): a common API specification, so you write a request once and run it against different model providers.
  - **OpenEnv** (Hugging Face): a hub for sharing reinforcement-learning *environments* (the simulated worlds agents practice in), packaged as containers that support the Gym interface.
  - **OpenSandbox** (Alibaba): isolated sandboxes, built on Docker and Kubernetes, for running agent code safely.
- **Background:** a *neutral foundation* owns a project's name and governance so no single company controls it, which makes rival companies more willing to adopt it. The foundation already hosts the Model Context Protocol (MCP).
- **Why it matters for you:** If you want to try RL beyond textbook examples, OpenEnv plus Gym-style environments is becoming a shared standard worth learning.

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Encoding Categorical Features

**In one sentence:** Most ML models only understand numbers, so categorical columns such as color, city or product type have to be turned into numbers in a way that doesn't invent fake meaning.

**Intuition:** Suppose you code jersey colors as red = 0, green = 1, blue = 2. A linear model now "believes" blue is twice as green as green and that green sits halfway between red and blue. That's nonsense. **One-hot encoding** fixes this by giving each category its own yes/no column, like a row of light switches where exactly one is on.

**Tiny example:** a column `["red", "green", "blue", "green"]` becomes three 0/1 columns:

| row | color_blue | color_green | color_red |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 2 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| 3 | 0 | 1 | 0 |

```python
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder
enc = OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")    # unseen categories -> all zeros
X_train_enc = enc.fit_transform(X_train[["color"]])
X_test_enc  = enc.transform(X_test[["color"]])  # fit on train only!
```

**When it's used:** almost every tabular project: linear and logistic regression, neural networks, and k-nearest neighbors. Tree models can often handle *ordinal* codes (integer labels) fine, and categories with a real order, such as small < medium < large, should be encoded in that order.

**Common pitfalls:**
- *IDs that look like numbers aren't numbers.* ZIP codes, phone numbers and product IDs should be read as strings (`pd.read_csv(..., dtype={"zip": str})`), or `01234` turns into `1234` and becomes a meaningless "quantity."
- *High cardinality.* One-hot encoding 10,000 user IDs creates 10,000 sparse columns. Group rare categories into an "other" bucket, or use target or learned embeddings instead.

**In today's news:** Story 6. pandas 3.1.0 began reading category columns as numbers, and postal codes lost their leading zeros.

### LLMs: RLHF (Learning from Preferences)

**In one sentence:** Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) fine-tunes an LLM to produce answers that people *prefer*, using a learned "reward model" as the scorekeeper.

**Intuition:** Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is like copying a model essay. RLHF is like a writing coach who never writes for you and only says "this draft is better than that one." After thousands of comparisons, you learn what "better" means.

**How it works (three steps):**
1. **SFT:** fine-tune the base model on example conversations.
2. **Reward model:** people compare pairs of answers, and a model learns to give the preferred one a higher score.
3. **RL:** the LLM generates answers, the reward model scores them, and an algorithm such as PPO nudges the LLM toward higher scores. A penalty keeps it from drifting too far from the SFT model.

**Tiny example (training the reward model):** answer A scores r_A = 2.0 and answer B scores r_B = 0.5. The predicted probability that people prefer A is σ(r_A − r_B) = σ(1.5) = 1 / (1 + e^−1.5) ≈ **0.818**. If people really did prefer A, the loss is −ln(0.818) ≈ **0.20**, which is small. If they preferred B, it is −ln(0.182) ≈ **1.70**, a large loss that pushes the scores to swap.

**When it's used:** making assistants helpful and polite, and refusing harmful requests. Simpler variants such as DPO skip the separate reward model, and "RL with verifiable rewards" swaps human preferences for automatic checks, such as whether a math answer is correct.

**Common pitfalls:**
- *Reward hacking:* the model learns to please the scorer (long, confident-sounding answers) rather than to be right.
- *Over-crediting RL:* abilities often come from pretraining or SFT, and RL only amplifies them.

**In today's news:** Story 5 found that the "Wait…" self-checks usually credited to RL were mostly inherited from earlier training stages.

### Deep Learning: Dropout

**In one sentence:** During training, dropout randomly switches off a fraction of a layer's neurons at each step, so the network can't depend too heavily on any single one.

**Intuition:** It's like a team where random members call in sick each day. Everyone has to learn enough to cover for others, so the team stays strong even when someone is missing. Each training step effectively trains a different "thinned" sub-network, and at test time the full network behaves like an average, or *ensemble*, of all of them.

**Tiny example ("inverted" dropout, p = 0.5):** activations `[2.0, 1.0, 3.0, 4.0]`, random keep-mask `[1, 0, 1, 0]`. The survivors are scaled by 1 / (1 − p) = 2, giving `[4.0, 0.0, 6.0, 0.0]`. Each value is kept half the time and doubled when kept, so its *expected* value is unchanged: 0.5 × 2 × 3.0 = 3.0. That's why no rescaling is needed at test time.

```python
import torch.nn as nn
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(100, 64), nn.ReLU(),
                      nn.Dropout(p=0.5),       # active only in train mode
                      nn.Linear(64, 10))
model.train()  # dropout ON
model.eval()   # dropout OFF for validation and inference
```

**When it's used:** fully connected layers in classic networks, and inside transformers, usually at low rates such as 0.1. Large LLMs pretrained on huge datasets often use little or no dropout because they rarely see the same data twice.

**Common pitfalls:**
- *Forgetting `model.eval()`:* predictions become random and accuracy looks mysteriously worse.
- *Too high a rate on a small network:* p = 0.5 everywhere can cause underfitting. Start low (0.1–0.3) and tune it on validation data.

**In today's news:** Story 1's MAI-DxO pools several AI "specialists" much like an ensemble. Dropout gets a similar averaging effect *inside* a single network.
