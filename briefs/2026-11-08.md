## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. Nous Research's Arete-27B: a fine-tune that removes most state propaganda from Qwen

- **What happened:** On Nov 6, Nous Research released Arete-27B, a version of Alibaba's open-weight Qwen3.8-27B that it fine-tuned to stop repeating Chinese government talking points on topics like free speech and surveillance.
- **Key details:**
  - Training was supervised fine-tuning (SFT) only: 28,886 examples, about 53M tokens, 2 epochs (today's deep learning concept). The example answers were written by Thinking Machines' open Inkling model, because closed models' terms ban using them to train other models.
  - Nous built an 803-prompt test across seven categories. For each prompt it listed 2–4 dated facts, and Grok 4.7 acted as judge (today's LLM concept), without knowing which model wrote each answer.
  - Self-reported results: propaganda fell from 22% of answers to 0.9%, and the factual score rose from 2.32 to 2.98 out of 5. In the free speech category, propaganda fell from 60% to 1%.
  - General skill held steady on Nous's internal Hermes Bench (65.60% vs 65.77% for the original). Weights, the test set and the training data are on Hugging Face under Apache 2.0.
- **Background:** **Supervised fine-tuning** means continuing to train a model on example prompt-and-answer pairs so it imitates those answers. It is cheaper than pretraining by many orders of magnitude.
- **The catch:** Nous wrote the test, picked the judge and ran the numbers. The judge's own political leanings could shape what counts as "propaganda," and the reasoning-mode results rest on only two runs so far.
- **Why it matters for you:** The fact that a few tens of millions of tokens can reshape a 27B model's behavior shows how thin some learned behaviors are. The evaluation setup (fact lists, a blinded judge, per-category scores) is a good template for any project where you measure model behavior rather than accuracy.
- **Source:** [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/state-propaganda-in-some-chinese-ai-models-is-only-a-thin-layer-that-fine-tuning-can-strip-away/)

### 2. Baidu's ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base: open weights, but not for Europe

- **What happened:** On Nov 6, Baidu posted ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base on Hugging Face, its first large open-weight model since summer 2025, under a license that forbids using it in the EU, UK and South Korea.
- **Key details:**
  - It is a mixture-of-experts model with 280B total parameters and about 14B active per token (5%), trained on 33.5 trillion tokens. Running it needs at least 288 GB of GPU memory.
  - Self-reported scores: it beats comparable base models on 24 of 30 tests, including MMLU 93.3 (DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base: 89.4) and HumanEval+ 82.3 (69.5).
  - License terms: products must show "Powered by ERNIE," companies over $50M in yearly revenue need written permission, and neither the model nor its outputs may be used to improve other AI models. The previous ERNIE 4.5 family used Apache 2.0.
- **Background:** A **base model** has only been pretrained to predict the next token. It hasn't been tuned to follow instructions or chat, so it is mostly useful for research and as a starting point for your own fine-tuning. Baidu says a chat version is coming.
- **The catch:** No independent benchmark results yet, and the article notes open questions about how the geographic ban would be enforced.
- **Why it matters for you:** Always read the license before building on a model. "Open weights" can still mean restrictions on where you are, how big your company is, and whether you may train on its outputs, which is the same trick Arete (Story 1) had to work around.
- **Source:** [Trending Topics](https://www.trendingtopics.eu/baidu-ernie-5-5-banned-europe-license/)

## Research Highlights

### 3. Evaluating a model on 2% of a benchmark: random, but picked well (arXiv:2611.00883)

- **What it is:** A Bosch Research and CMU paper, posted Oct 31 and trending on Hugging Face this week, proposes BestRand, a cheap way to shrink a benchmark to a small subset whose scores still match the full benchmark.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Run a few cheap "probe" models (5 by default) on a pool of benchmark questions and note their average score.
  - Draw 128 random candidate subsets of the size you want, and keep the one where the probes' average score is closest to their average on the whole pool.
  - Evaluate your real models only on that subset. Because probe scores are cached, picking the subset costs no extra model calls.
- **Key details:**
  - Tested on 70 benchmarks (44 text, 16 multimodal, 10 agent), keeping between 2% and 50% of the questions.
  - Average error fell 16.4% compared with plain random sampling and two methods based on item response theory (TinyBenchmarks, ZipBench). At 2% retention on text benchmarks, average error was 6.27 points versus 7.37 for plain random.
  - Under distribution shift (new model families, newer models), learned methods sometimes did worse than plain random. BestRand cut that error by 21.8% on average.
- **The catch:** With hundreds of past model results to learn from, the learned methods catch up. BestRand's biggest edge is when you have little data about past models.
- **Why it matters for you:** A strong, simple random baseline is hard to beat, and the trick here is the same one behind today's ML concept: make your small sample look like the population on something you can measure. Code is on GitHub.
- **Sources:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00883) · [GitHub](https://github.com/George-Ling3/BestRand)

### 4. Benchmarks need maintenance like software (arXiv:2611.08417)

- **What it is:** A Nov 5 paper from Stanford (including Christopher Ré) argues that agent benchmarks quietly decay after release and need version numbers, changelogs and regular re-checks.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Over time, models find valid answers the grader rejects, and changes to software packages or servers break task assumptions. The authors call the build-up "benchmark maintenance debt."
  - They used AI auditors to propose fixes and had humans approve every change. The audit flagged 28 of Terminal-Bench 2.0's 89 tasks (about a third), released as Terminal-Bench 2.1.
- **Key details:**
  - The most common defect was a mismatch between the instructions and what the grader checks (16 tasks), followed by task drift (12) and environment problems (8). Some tasks had more than one.
  - After the fixes, individual scores moved by up to 12 points and leaderboard positions by up to 3 places, though the overall ranking barely changed (Spearman correlation 0.958 across 19 agents).
  - Timeouts caused more than half of failed attempts. Giving one model more time closed a 30-point gap between two agent setups to 5 points.
  - Two strong AI auditors agreed on the main defect type with Cohen's kappa 0.80 (covered Nov 5).
- **The catch:** Most evidence comes from one benchmark, and the auditor workflow was scored against a release it helped build.
- **Why it matters for you:** A leaderboard number depends on the benchmark version and on settings like time limits. When you compare models, report both, and treat time and token budgets as part of the experiment.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08417)

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Stratified Sampling

**In one sentence:** Stratified sampling splits data into groups (strata) and samples from each group in proportion, so a small sample keeps the same mix as the full dataset.

**Intuition:** If you poll 100 students at a school that is 30% first-years, a random draw might give you 20 or 40 first-years by luck. Stratifying means you deliberately pick exactly 30 first-years and 70 others.

**Formula / Tiny example:** A fraud dataset has 1,000 rows, 50 of them fraud (5%). A random 20% test set (200 rows) should hold 10 fraud cases on average, but the count has a standard deviation of about sqrt(200 × 0.05 × 0.95) = sqrt(9.5) ≈ 3.1, so getting anywhere from about 4 to 16 is normal. With 4, your recall estimate (from Precision and Recall) rests on just four examples. Stratifying gives exactly 10 fraud and 190 normal rows every time.

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, StratifiedKFold
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y, random_state=0)
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
```

**When it's used:** Train/test splits and cross-validation with imbalanced classes, surveys and polls, and building small evaluation sets that represent every category.

**Common pitfalls:** Forgetting `stratify=y`, then getting a fold with almost no minority examples. Also, stratifying on the label does not protect you from time-based or group-based leakage; for those, split by time or by group instead.

**In today's news:** BestRand (Story 3) uses a related idea: among many random subsets, keep the one whose probe-model scores best match the full pool, so the small sample stays representative.

### LLMs: LLM-as-a-Judge

**In one sentence:** LLM-as-a-judge means using one language model to grade the outputs of another, in place of (or alongside) human raters.

**Intuition:** A teacher with 500 essays to mark hands a detailed rubric to a capable teaching assistant. It's much faster, but the teacher should still spot-check grades, because the assistant has its own habits and blind spots.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Before trusting a judge, measure it against people. Suppose humans labeled 200 answers "pass" or "fail," and the judge agrees on 170: agreement is 170 / 200 = 85%. Then check **position bias**: show the judge 100 pairs of answers in order A,B and again in order B,A. If it picks the same answer both times for 78 pairs, it flips on 22%, a sign it favors whichever answer comes first.

```python
prompt = f"""Facts: {facts}
Answer: {answer}
Does the answer state, omit, or contradict each fact?
Reply in JSON: {{"fact_1": "...", "score_1_to_5": ...}}"""
```

**When it's used:** Grading open-ended answers in benchmarks, comparing chatbots, filtering synthetic training data, and auditing benchmark tasks.

**Common pitfalls:** Judges tend to prefer longer answers, the first answer shown, and answers from their own model family. Give them a specific rubric or fact list, hide which model wrote what, and report agreement with humans (for example Cohen's kappa) alongside the scores.

**In today's news:** Arete's propaganda test used Grok 4.7 as a blinded judge checking answers against fact lists (Story 1), and the Terminal-Bench audit used AI auditors whose agreement was measured with kappa (Story 4).

### Deep Learning: Epochs, Batches and Iterations

**In one sentence:** An **epoch** is one full pass over the training data, a **batch** is the chunk of examples used for a single weight update, and an **iteration** (or step) is one such update.

**Intuition:** Studying a 900-card deck of flashcards 32 cards at a time: each group of 32 is a batch, studying a group and adjusting your memory is one iteration, and getting through the whole deck once is an epoch.

**Formula / Tiny example:** steps per epoch = ceil(N / batch size). Arete was trained on N = 28,886 examples for 2 epochs. With a batch size of 32: 28,886 / 32 = 902.7, so 903 steps per epoch (902 full batches plus a final batch of 22), and 2 × 903 = 1,806 steps in total. Its ~53M tokens over 2 epochs is about 26.5M tokens per pass, or about 917 tokens per example.

```python
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader
loader = DataLoader(train_ds, batch_size=32, shuffle=True)
print(len(loader))                   # 903 batches per epoch
for epoch in range(2):
    for xb, yb in loader:            # one iteration per batch
        loss = loss_fn(model(xb), yb)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
```

**When it's used:** Every training loop. Learning-rate schedules, checkpoints and logging are usually set in steps or epochs.

**Common pitfalls:** Changing the batch size without adjusting the learning rate or step count, then comparing runs that saw different numbers of updates. Also, training too many epochs on a small fine-tuning set: the model starts memorizing, so watch validation loss each epoch and stop when it rises. LLM fine-tunes often use only 1–3 epochs.

**In today's news:** Arete-27B was fine-tuned for 2 epochs on 28,886 examples (Story 1).
