- **Key details:**

- **Why it matters for you:** Price per token and cost per task are different numbers. A model that writes longer answers can cost more even at a lower list price, so measure what your actual tasks cost.

## Research Highlights

### 3. The winner's curse in LLM leaderboards (arXiv:2611.22227)

- **What happened:** In a paper listed Nov 18, Sikun Xu, Raphael Thomadsen, Dennis J. Zhang and Heng Zhang showed that the top scores on public LLM leaderboards are systematically too high, and proposed a fix.
- **Key details:**
  - Across 14 benchmarks from 5 families, the first-place score needed to be lowered by about 0.09 to 3.86 percentage points.
  - On SWE-bench Verified, the tied leaders' scores dropped by 0.90 points, enough to make "who's #1" statistically shaky.
  - Their method put 13 of 173 SWE-bench Verified models in the leading tier, meaning the data can't reliably separate those 13.
  - They suggest leaderboards publish a "RULER" report (rank ranges plus adjusted scores) next to raw rankings.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Every benchmark score has some luck in it. Whoever lands at the top usually had real skill *and* good luck, so their score overstates their skill.
  - The authors resample the benchmark tasks many times (a bootstrap) to estimate how much of the top score is luck, then subtract it.
- **Why it matters for you:** The same thing happens when you try 20 models or hyperparameter settings and report the best validation score. See today's ML concept.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22227)

### 4. Counting wrong labels after a network has memorized them (arXiv:2611.22449)

- **What happened:** In a paper listed Nov 18, Yuchen Li and four co-authors proposed a way to estimate how many training labels are wrong using only one already-trained model.
- **Key details:**
  - On CIFAR-10N's noisiest human labels, after the model had fit 99–100% of its wrong labels, the method separated wrong from right labels with AUC 0.94–0.96, vs. 0.73–0.76 for methods based on loss.
  - On a vision transformer trained on CIFAR-10 with 8–58% label noise, methods based on predictions estimated 0–6% noise. The new audit recovered 54–96% of the true rate, and at least 93% of the examples it flagged were truly mislabeled.
  - On a briefly trained model, its estimate was within 1.7 points of the true noise rate. Auditing 5,000 examples took 14–27 seconds on one GPU.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Once a network has memorized a wrong label, its loss on that example is small, so loss-based checks miss it.
  - Instead, the authors look at the *pattern* of each example's gradient (how strongly it pulls on different parts of the model) and ask whether it looks more like a different class's typical pattern. If so, the label is flagged.
- **The catch:** An existing method, confident learning, separated wrong labels about as well and usually estimated the noise rate more closely, though it trains four extra models and had lower precision.
- **Why it matters for you:** Real datasets have label errors, and a model with 100% training accuracy hides them. Today's deep learning concept shows why.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22449)

### 5. Removing data delays a skill, but doesn't prevent it (arXiv:2611.24844)

- **What happened:** In a paper submitted Nov 18, Márk Marosi and Péter Antal tested what happens when specific training data is withheld so a model won't learn a skill.
- **Key details:**
  - They withheld individual skills from models trained on chess, Othello, molecule strings, bracket languages and web text.
  - A chess model never trained to respond to "check" ranked legal responses almost as well as one that was.
  - When the withheld data was added later, models learned the skill from a small fraction of the data needed when starting from scratch, and that fraction shrank as models improved. A control rule of similar difficulty was learned much more slowly.
  - The same pattern held for a programming language withheld from a 7-billion-parameter model's training text.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - General knowledge picked up from the rest of the data does part of the work the missing data would have done.
  - So filtering buys time, and the time it buys can be predicted from smaller models.
- **Why it matters for you:** Deleting data is a common safety and privacy tool. This paper suggests checking what a model can infer, not just what it was shown. See today's LLM concept.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24844)

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: The Winner's Curse (Why the Top Score Is Usually Too High)

**In one sentence:** When you pick the best of several noisy scores, the winner's score tends to overestimate how well it will do on new data, because you selected for good luck as well as skill.

**Intuition:** Ten equally good free-throw shooters each take 20 shots. Whoever scores highest probably had a lucky day. Ask them to shoot again and they'll likely drop back toward the group average.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Ten models all have a true accuracy of 80%, each tested on the same-sized set of 500 examples. One score's standard error is √(0.8 × 0.2 / 500) ≈ 1.79 points. The expected maximum of 10 independent normal draws is about 1.54 standard errors above the mean, so the best model is expected to score about 80 + 1.54 × 1.79 ≈ **82.7%**, with no real advantage at all.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
scores = rng.binomial(500, 0.8, size=(200_000, 10)) / 500
best = scores.max(axis=1)
print(best.mean())          # ≈ 0.827
print((best >= 0.82).mean())  # ≈ 0.79: the "winner" scores 82%+ most of the time
```

**When it's used:** Model selection, hyperparameter search, Kaggle leaderboards, A/B tests with many variants, and picking "top" stocks or features.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Reporting the validation score of the model you chose as its expected performance. Use a separate test set that you touch once, after selection.
- Treating a 0.5-point lead as real when the gap is smaller than the noise. Add confidence intervals or rank ranges.

**In today's news:** Story 3 measures exactly this effect on public LLM leaderboards.

### LLMs: Pretraining Data Filtering

**In one sentence:** Pretraining data filtering removes documents from a model's training corpus, usually with keyword rules or a classifier, to improve quality or to keep the model from learning certain content.

**Intuition:** It's like editing a textbook to remove one chapter. Students who learned everything around it can often work out much of the missing chapter themselves.

**Formula / Tiny example:** A corpus has 10,000,000 documents, and 0.2% of them (20,000) cover a topic you want removed. Your filter catches 95% of those and wrongly removes 1% of everything else.
- Topic documents removed: 0.95 × 20,000 = 19,000. **1,000 slip through.**
- Innocent documents removed: 0.01 × 9,980,000 = **99,800**, about five times as many as the target documents removed.
- So even a "95% accurate" filter leaves some target data in and throws out a lot of good data.

**When it's used:** Removing spam, duplicates and low-quality web pages; removing personal data; and keeping hazardous knowledge (for example, detailed weapons information) out of models.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Assuming the model can't do something because the data was removed. Related knowledge can fill the gap.
- Ignoring false positives: aggressive filters can remove dialects or topics unevenly and bias the model.

**In today's news:** Story 5 found that withheld skills come back from a small fraction of the data as models improve elsewhere.

### Deep Learning: Memorizing Noisy Labels

**In one sentence:** A large enough neural network can fit every training label, including the wrong ones, but it tends to learn the correct, consistent patterns first and memorize the mislabeled examples later.

**Intuition:** A student studying from flashcards, a few of which have wrong answers, first learns the general rules. Only after many passes do they also memorize the odd cards that contradict the rules.

**Formula / Tiny example:** We flipped 20% of the labels (419 of 2,000) in a synthetic dataset and trained a two-layer network, measuring how many training labels it reproduces in each group:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier
X, y = make_classification(n_samples=2000, n_features=20, n_informative=10, random_state=0)
noisy = np.random.default_rng(0).random(len(y)) < 0.2
y_train = np.where(noisy, 1 - y, y)          # flip 20% of labels
clf = MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(256, 256), random_state=0)
for epoch in range(1, 101):
    clf.partial_fit(X, y_train, classes=[0, 1])
# check (clf.predict(X) == y_train) separately for clean and noisy rows
```

| Epoch | Clean labels fit | Wrong labels fit |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 93.4% | 19.6% |
| 20 | 97.4% | 50.4% |
| 100 | 100% | 100% |

By epoch 100, training accuracy is a perfect 100%, and the wrong labels are invisible in the loss.

**When it's used:** Understanding why early stopping helps on noisy data, and as the basis for "small-loss" label cleaning, which flags examples that still have high loss early in training.

**Common pitfalls:**
- Reading 100% training accuracy as "the data is clean." It may mean the model memorized the errors.
- Checking for label errors only on the final model, after the evidence has disappeared.

**In today's news:** Story 4 builds a label audit that still works after this memorization has happened.
