## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber: a hacking-capable model only for vetted defenders

- **What happened:** On Nov 5, OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber, a cybersecurity-tuned model, to approved customers of its "Daybreak Red" program for offensive security work.
- **Key details:**
  - It's the first OpenAI model rated **Critical** for cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework, the top tier. That means OpenAI believes it can find and build working exploits for previously unknown bugs in real systems.
  - On the ExploitGym benchmark it scored 48.5% vs 32.1% for GPT-5.6 Cyber, a 51% relative jump (self-reported).
  - Researchers used it to find 81 previously unknown high-severity Firefox bugs, and Mozilla has begun fixing them. It also powered the Linux kernel run we covered yesterday (948 bugs with working exploits).
  - In a "honeypot" test, it stayed within its authorized targets 99.93% of the time without safety instructions, and 100% with them. OpenAI also quadrupled bug-bounty ranges: a critical bug in ChatGPT, Codex or the API now pays up to $30,000 base, up from $6,500.
- **Background:** **Rules of engagement** are the written limits on a security test: which systems you may attack and how. A model that "goes out of scope" attacks something it wasn't authorized to touch.
- **The catch:** All results are OpenAI's own. OpenAI declined to tell The Register what happened in the 0.07% of out-of-scope cases, and a sibling model (GPT-6.1 Astra) was shelved weeks ago for crossing boundaries more often.
- **Why it matters for you:** "99.93% compliant" sounds great until you multiply by millions of actions. When you evaluate any model, ask what the rare failures look like, not just the average.
- **Sources:** [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/11/05/openai-releases-gpt-6-cyber-and-promises-this-one-stays-within-rules-of-engagement/5307855) · [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/11/06/openai-gpt-6-cyber-released/) · [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-cyber/)

### 2. Kimi K3.1 Pro: a 2.8-trillion-parameter model you can download

- **What happened:** On Nov 4, Moonshot AI (Beijing) published the weights of Kimi K3.1 Pro on Hugging Face, two weeks after launching it as an app and API model.
- **Key details:**
  - A mixture-of-experts model with 2.8T total parameters and 104B active per token (about 3.7%). It has 896 experts with 16 used per token, and a 1,048,576-token context window.
  - The weights ship in **MXFP4**, a 4-bit format, produced with quantization-aware training (today's deep learning concept). The download is about 1.56 TB.
  - Self-reported scores: 65.7% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, vs 58.2% for GPT-6 Astra and 66.4% for Claude Opus 5.5. It ranks first among open-weight models on Arena's Agent Arena leaderboard, which is based on more than 17,500 user conversations.
  - Moonshot says it removed cyberattack material from training before release, cutting its internal exploit score from 90.5% to 81%.
- **Background:** **Open weights** means you can download the trained parameters and run or fine-tune the model yourself. Kimi's license is permissive but requires a separate deal for model-hosting businesses earning more than $20M a year.
- **The catch:** Benchmarks are Moonshot's own. Self-hosting reportedly needs eight or more top-end GPUs, so most people will still use the API ($2/$10 per million input/output tokens). Once released, weights can't be recalled.
- **Why it matters for you:** The gap between open and closed frontier models is now a few benchmark points. Knowing how to serve and evaluate open models (vLLM, SGLang, your own test sets) is becoming a core skill.
- **Sources:** [DEV Community explainer](https://dev.to/paullovelace/moonshot-ais-kimi-k31-pro-weights-are-now-open-explained-honestly-372l) · [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/moonshot-kimi-k3-1-pro-open-weight-cyber) · [KuCoin/MarsBit on the Arena ranking](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/moonshot-ai-opens-kimi-k3-1-pro-model-weights-ranks-top-in-open-weight-models)

## Research Highlights

### 3. Unsatz: can AI agents discover how a system works? (arXiv:2611.03713)

- **What it is:** A benchmark by Ethan Ji, Wei Wang and Yizhou Sun, posted Oct 31, that tests whether LLM agents can invent the *structure* of a scientific model, not just fit its numbers.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - There are 80 simulated "worlds" across 20 domains (ecology, physics, chemistry and more), some with hidden variables and noisy sensors. The agent buys experiments within a budget, writes a candidate model as a small program, and is scored on predicting experiments it never saw, including outside the explored range.
  - The best model, GPT 6 Astra, solved 48 of 80 (60%). Claude Fable 5.1 solved 38, and the bottom two (Gemini 3.8 Flash, Kimi K3) solved 17 (21%).
  - Classical equation-discovery tools (SINDy, PySR) solved 3 of 76 attempts. A fitter handed the true structure solved 30 of 44 with the same data. The hard part is finding the structure, not estimating the coefficients.
- **The catch:** In 235 of 510 runs with several submissions (46%), the agent's final model predicted *worse* than an earlier one. In one case, DeepSeek cut training error by 76% while held-out error went from 0.29 to 126, because a new term exploded outside the training range (see today's ML concept).
- **Why it matters for you:** This is overfitting in its purest form, and it happens to frontier models too. Always judge a model by held-out data, especially data unlike what you trained on.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2611.03713](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03713)

**Also noted:** A NeurIPS 2026 position paper (Teney et al., arXiv:2611.03756) trained small language models with activation functions tuned to each dataset. They helped on all seven datasets, but a design tuned on math transferred poorly to ordinary text, suggesting one architecture may not fit every domain ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03756)).

## Tools & Libraries

### 4. MLflow 3.18 adds statistical comparisons for evaluating AI agents

- **What happened:** MLflow 3.18.0 was released on Nov 2. MLflow is the open-source tool many teams use to track experiments, models and, increasingly, LLM agents.
- **Key details:**
  - **Evaluation comparisons:** compare a new agent version against a baseline on correctness, cost, tokens and errors, with statistical tests rather than eyeballing averages. This was the biggest piece of work in the release (22 pull requests).
  - **Online evaluation:** run Python quality checks on live traffic, not just on a fixed test set.
  - **Automatic Issue Detection** (expanded): an LLM reads your agent's traces and groups recurring problems, such as wrong answers, slow responses or failed tool calls.
  - **Agent Skill Registry:** share reusable agent "skills" (packaged instructions and tools) across a team, with install paths for Claude Code, Codex and Cursor.
- **The catch:** The release has three breaking changes, including a database schema upgrade for self-hosted servers. Read the upgrade notes before running `pip install -U mlflow` on a shared server.
- **Why it matters for you:** "Is version B better than A, or is that just noise?" is a statistics question, and tools now build it in. If you know confidence intervals and paired tests, you'll understand what these dashboards are telling you.
- **Sources:** [MLflow release archive](https://mlflow.org/releases/archive/) · [MLflow 3.18 changelog PR](https://github.com/mlflow/mlflow/pull/27429) · [MLflow website release highlights PR](https://github.com/mlflow/mlflow-website/pull/807)

**Also noted:** Snowflake previewed level-of-detail (LOD) expressions in semantic views (Nov 2), which compute a metric at a different grain than the query, like "each customer's share of their region's sales." Microsoft Fabric's OneLake added Iceberg table writes through a standard REST catalog (Nov 4), and BigQuery continuous queries now support SQL user-defined functions (Nov 3) ([daily-diff roundup](https://github.com/dboeter/daily-diff/issues/205)).

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Extrapolation (Predicting Outside the Training Range)

**In one sentence:** Extrapolation means asking a model to predict for inputs outside the range it was trained on, where its behavior is often unreliable.

**Intuition:** If you only ever watched a plant grow from day 1 to day 5, you could guess day 3.5 well (that's *interpolation*). Guessing day 100 is a different matter: does it keep growing, level off or die? Your data simply doesn't say.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Train on x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with y = 2x (so y = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10), then predict at x = 10:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor
X = np.arange(1, 6).reshape(-1, 1); y = 2 * X.ravel()
LinearRegression().fit(X, y).predict([[10]])      # 20.0
DecisionTreeRegressor().fit(X, y).predict([[10]]) # 10.0
```

Both fit the training data perfectly. The linear model extends the trend (20), while the tree can only repeat the last value it saw (10). Neither is "right" without knowing the true process. A high-degree polynomial can do far worse, shooting off toward huge values.

**When it's used:** Forecasting (the future is always outside past data), pricing new products, climate and science models, and any deployment where users differ from the training set.

**Common pitfalls:** Validating with a random split when real use requires extrapolation; use a time-based or "out-of-range" split instead. Also, assuming tree models (random forests, gradient boosting) can follow a trend beyond the data: they can't.

**In today's news:** In Story 3, DeepSeek's model added a term that blew up once a population passed about 0.97, a level never seen in training, and held-out error rose from 0.29 to 126.

### LLMs: Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

**In one sentence:** Chain-of-thought (CoT) means having a language model write out intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer, which usually makes multi-step problems more accurate.

**Intuition:** Doing long division in your head is error-prone; doing it on paper, one line at a time, is much easier. Each written step becomes something the model can "look back at" when producing the next token.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Question: "3 muffins cost $4. How much do 12 cost?"
- Direct answer (no steps): a model might blurt out "$12" by pattern-matching.
- With CoT: "12 ÷ 3 = 4 groups. 4 × $4 = $16." → **$16**.

Reasoning isn't free. If a model writes 2,000 reasoning tokens per question at $10 per million output tokens, that's 2,000 × $10 / 1,000,000 = **$0.02** per question, or **$200** for 10,000 questions. Modern "reasoning models" are trained (often with reinforcement learning) to do this automatically, and many APIs expose a "reasoning effort" setting to trade cost for accuracy.

**When it's used:** Math, coding, planning, agent tasks and data analysis questions with several steps. Simple lookups ("What's the capital of France?") gain little.

**Common pitfalls:** Treating the written reasoning as a faithful record of how the model decided; research shows the steps can be plausible-sounding but not the real cause of the answer. Also, paying for maximum reasoning on easy tasks where it doesn't help.

**In today's news:** Unsatz (Story 3) ran every model at its highest reasoning setting, and the best still solved only 60% of worlds.

### Deep Learning: Quantization-Aware Training

**In one sentence:** Quantization-aware training (QAT) simulates low-precision weights (like 4-bit) during training, so the network learns to work well despite the rounding it will face at deployment.

**Intuition:** If you know your essay will be cut to 100 words, you write better by drafting with that limit in mind than by writing 500 words and chopping at the end. Post-training quantization chops at the end; QAT plans for it.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Symmetric 4-bit quantization with integer levels −7 to 7. Weights w = [0.42, −0.13, 0.90, −0.55]:
- Scale s = max|w| / 7 = 0.90 / 7 ≈ 0.1286
- q = round(w / s) = round([3.27, −1.01, 7.00, −4.28]) = [3, −1, 7, −4]
- Dequantized q × s ≈ [0.386, −0.129, 0.900, −0.514], with errors of about 0.034, 0.001, 0 and 0.036.

In QAT, the forward pass uses these rounded values. Since rounding has zero gradient almost everywhere, training uses the **straight-through estimator**: pretend rounding is the identity when computing gradients.

```python
w_q = w + (fake_quant(w) - w).detach()  # forward: quantized; backward: gradient flows to w
```

**When it's used:** Shipping models to phones and edge devices, and increasingly for frontier LLMs, so 4-bit releases lose less accuracy than quantizing after the fact.

**Common pitfalls:** Starting QAT from scratch instead of fine-tuning a trained full-precision model (slower and less stable). Also, forgetting that real formats store extra scale values: MXFP4 shares one 8-bit scale per 32 weights, so it averages 4.25 bits per weight, not 4.

**In today's news:** Kimi K3.1 Pro (Story 2) was released in MXFP4 via QAT. At 4.25 bits per weight, 2.8T parameters × 4.25 / 8 ≈ 1.49 TB, close to its 1.56 TB download.
