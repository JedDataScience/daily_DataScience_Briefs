## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. A frontier model edited a small model's weights directly and fixed its math

- **What happened:** On Nov 2, Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab of MIT CSAIL described "weight-space programming," in which OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra reads another network's weights and writes changes to them itself.
- **Key details:**
  - **Setup:** Astra got Python access to the weights of Alibaba's open Qwen3.5-0.8B model, some practice questions and a limited budget of test runs. It found the edit by inspecting and testing the model, not by gradient-based training.
  - **Results:** MATH-500 went from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1% and IFEval (instruction following) from 21.0% to 62.3%.
  - **How small the edit was:** 131,072 weights in parts of layers 15 and 19, about 0.016% of the model. The share of math answers that ran out of tokens fell from 93.0% to 33.1%.
  - **Why it worked:** The authors say the edit removed a direction in the model's internal numbers that was linked to repeating itself (see today's LLM concept). It fixed a failure; it didn't teach new math.
- **The catch:** The same approach gave no clear gain on Google's Gemma 3 270M, which doesn't have that repetition problem. The best run took 20.3 hours. The paper has no fine-tuning baseline, and no one has reproduced it yet.
- **Why it matters for you:** A big jump on a benchmark can come from fixing one failure mode, such as endless repetition, rather than from new ability. Before you celebrate a score, look at what kinds of errors went away.
- **Sources:** [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/like-gene-editing-for-neural-networks-mit-researchers-used-gpt-6-astra-to-edit-qwens-open-weights-directly-boosting-math-performance) · [Authors' blog post](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/)

**Also noted:** Meituan launched LongCat-2.5 on Nov 2, a 1.6-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with about 48B parameters active per token. It's available through Meituan's chat app and API. Weights are planned but not out yet, and Meituan hasn't published benchmark scores ([TechNode via AF.net](https://af.net/realtime/meituan-launches-1-6t-parameter-longcat-2-5-multimodal-model/)).

## Research Highlights

### 2. Plain RAG beat 11 dedicated agent-memory systems (arXiv:2610.34073)

- **What it is:** "Memory Tax: Dissecting the Net Value of Agent Memory," a preprint from a UIUC-led team (including Jiawei Han and ChengXiang Zhai) that trended on Hugging Face on Oct 28.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Chat assistants often add a "memory" system that summarizes or reorganizes past conversations. The baseline is to keep the raw chat history and search it with ordinary RAG (retrieval-augmented generation).
  - The authors tested 11 memory systems (such as A-Mem, MemoryOS and HippoRAG2) on 3 benchmarks with 3 answering models. In 90 of the 99 combinations, the memory system scored below plain RAG.
  - They call the loss a **memory tax**. It has three causes: details get lost when memories are compressed, the right evidence is harder to find, and results change when a different model reads the memory.
- **Key numbers:** On LongMemEval-s with Qwen3.6-27B, plain RAG scored 81.4, A-Mem 79.4 and OpenViking 58.8. Their fix is to also search the raw history and rerank both sets of results. It raised SimpleMem from 73.6 to 82.8.
- **The catch:** It's a preprint. Some benchmarks were run on subsets to save money, and the strongest model tested was GPT-5.6 Luna (low).
- **Why it matters for you:** This is today's ML concept in action. A fancy system has to beat a simple baseline that gets the same context budget, and you should report its cost next to its accuracy.
- **Sources:** [explainx.ai](https://explainx.ai/blog/memory-tax-agent-memory-systems-underperform-naive-rag-uiuc-paper-2026) · [The Agent Report](https://the-agent-report.com/2026/10/memory-tax-agent-memory-loses-naive-rag/)

### 3. Retrieval with no search index: let the model walk a table of contents (arXiv:2610.35868)

- **What it is:** "Retriever-Free Retrieval: Navigating Documents with Decision Models" (DecisionRAG), posted Oct 26 by Chao Wan, Kilian Q. Weinberger and co-authors.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Normal RAG turns text into embeddings (lists of numbers) and finds the nearest ones. If that first search misses, the LLM never sees the right passage.
  - DecisionRAG organizes documents into a tree, from broad topics down to paragraphs, like library shelves. At each level a language model scores all the branches in one pass, without generating text, and goes down the best ones.
  - It needs no training for the specific task and no first-stage retriever.
- **Key numbers (self-reported):** Compared with an 8B embedding model plus an 8B reranker, recall@1 on MultiDoc2Dial rose from 33.6% to 47.4% (+13.8 points). Hit@1 on Natural Questions rose from 48.0% to 65.4% (+17.4). Median time was 0.68 to 1.76 seconds per query, and API cost was 0.22 to 2.33 cents.
- **The catch:** If the model prunes the wrong branch, those paragraphs can't be reached. The cost of building the tree isn't reported, and no code has been released.
- **Why it matters for you:** Embedding search isn't the only way to retrieve text. For documents that already have structure, such as manuals or legal codes, walking that structure can work better.
- **Sources:** [deeplearn.org (arXiv listing)](https://deeplearn.org/arxiv/857267/retriever-free-retrieval:-navigating-documents-with-decision-models) · [SmartScope](https://smartscope.blog/en/blog/decisionrag-document-search-accuracy-without-per-query-retrievers-2026/)

**Also noted:** OpenAI's claimed disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis (covered Oct 27) now has a detailed walkthrough. CMU's Ryan O'Donnell posted it, with a note that it was produced with help from LLMs. It explains the argument but doesn't say whether the proof is correct ([CMU PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~odonnell/some_eth_false_exposition.pdf)).

### 4. DeepSeek open-sources a prototype coding agent that watches your repo and team chat

- **What happened:** On Oct 31, DeepSeek released `dsh-superagent-prototype` on GitHub under the MIT license. It's a "proactive" coding agent built on its DeepSeek Harness (dsh).
- **Key details:**
  - **How it works:** It watches one GitHub repository and, if you want, Feishu group chats (Feishu is a workplace chat app). When an authorized person asks for something, it opens a work item, does the work in its own session and folder, and replies in the same thread, for example with a pull request.
  - **Human control:** A web panel lists the items that need a person to decide.
  - **Scope:** DeepSeek calls it a research prototype, not an official product, and says it differs from the version it uses internally.
- **Background:** The **MIT license** is a permissive open-source license. You can use, change and sell the code as long as you keep the copyright notice.
- **The catch:** It handles one repository at a time. Some ideas in DeepSeek's write-up aren't in the released code yet.
- **Why it matters for you:** It's a small, readable example of agent design: a queue of work items, isolated workspaces and human approval steps. Read the code before you trust any agent with write access to your repo.
- **Sources:** [DEV Community](https://dev.to/paullovelace/deepseek-super-agent-a-proactive-open-source-coding-agent-prototype-what-it-actually-is-and-438a) · [Shiftdelete](https://shiftdelete.net/en/deepseek-coding-agent)

- **Key details:**
  - **What it adds:** Testing agents before deployment, ongoing monitoring of their behavior, traces of each decision and tool call, and alerts for drift and hallucinations. It also tracks token spending.

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Baseline Models

**In one sentence:** A baseline is the simplest reasonable model for a task, and any new model has to beat it to be worth using.

**Intuition:** Before you praise a weather app, compare it with "tomorrow will be like today." If the app barely beats that, it isn't adding much.

**Formula / Tiny example:** You have 1,000 emails and 900 are not spam. A "model" that always says "not spam" is right 900/1,000 = 90% of the time. If your classifier gets 91%, it only fixes 10 of the 100 mistakes the baseline makes.

```python
from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

base = DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent")
print(cross_val_score(base, X, y, cv=5).mean())   # about 0.90 here
print(cross_val_score(my_model, X, y, cv=5).mean())
```

For regression, use `DummyRegressor(strategy="mean")` and compare mean absolute error.

**When it's used:** At the start of every project; in papers ("compared with what?"); for time series (predict last value); and for search (keyword or plain embedding retrieval).

**Common pitfalls:** Comparing with a weak baseline, or not giving it the same data or budget. Forgetting cost: a model that is 1 point better but 30 times more expensive may not be worth it.

**In today's news:** In story 2, plain RAG over raw chat history was the baseline, and it beat dedicated memory systems in 90 of 99 settings.

### LLMs: Steering Vectors and Directional Ablation

**In one sentence:** Some behaviors of an LLM line up with a single direction in its internal activations, and you can add or remove that direction to change the behavior.

**Intuition:** Think of a mixing desk. If one slider controls "repeat yourself," you can turn it down without touching the others.

**Formula / Tiny example:** Average the hidden activations when the model repeats itself, say r = [2, 1, 0], and when it doesn't, n = [1, 1, 0]. The difference d = r − n = [1, 0, 0] is the direction. To remove it from a new activation h = [3, 2, 1], subtract its projection: h − (h·d)d = [3, 2, 1] − 3·[1, 0, 0] = [0, 2, 1].

```python
import numpy as np
d = r.mean(0) - n.mean(0)          # repeat minus normal
d = d / np.linalg.norm(d)          # make it unit length
h_clean = h - (h @ d) * d          # remove that direction
```

Directional ablation does the same thing to the weights, so the model can't write in that direction at all.

**When it's used:** Interpretability research, reducing repetition or refusals, and steering tone or topic without retraining.

**Common pitfalls:** A direction found from a few examples may also carry other meanings, so removing it can break things. Always test on held-out tasks, not just the behavior you targeted.

**In today's news:** Story 1's edit worked by removing a direction linked to repetition.

### Deep Learning: Counting Parameters

**In one sentence:** A network's parameters are its learnable numbers (weights and biases), and you can count them layer by layer.

**Intuition:** Every connection between two neurons is one adjustable knob. Count the knobs.

**Formula / Tiny example:**
- A linear layer from 512 inputs to 256 outputs has 512 × 256 = 131,072 weights plus 256 biases = **131,328** parameters.
- A 3×3 convolution from 64 to 128 channels has 3 × 3 × 64 × 128 = 73,728 weights plus 128 biases = **73,856**.

```python
import torch.nn as nn
layer = nn.Linear(512, 256)
print(sum(p.numel() for p in layer.parameters()))  # 131328
```

Memory: 0.8B parameters × 2 bytes each (bf16) ≈ 1.6 GB just to store the weights.

**When it's used:** Choosing a model that fits your GPU, comparing architectures and reading model cards ("1.6T total, 48B active" means about 3% of parameters are used per token).

**Common pitfalls:** Forgetting biases or the embedding table. In small LLMs the embeddings can be a big share: a 150,000-token vocabulary × 1,024 dimensions is 153.6M parameters. Also, don't mix up parameter count with training memory, which also needs gradients and optimizer state.

**In today's news:** Story 1 changed 131,072 weights out of about 0.8B, which is 131,072 / 800,000,000 ≈ 0.016%. LongCat-2.5 (Also noted, AI & ML) uses 48B of its 1.6T parameters per token.
