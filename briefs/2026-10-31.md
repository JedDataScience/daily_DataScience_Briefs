## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. OpenAI rebuilds its Model Spec around five values

- **What happened:** On Oct 29, OpenAI published a new version of its Model Spec, the public document that describes how its models are supposed to behave and how they should handle conflicting instructions.
- **Key details:**
  - **Five values:** faithfulness to human intent, harm minimization, honesty and transparency, human agency, and care. Faithfulness to intent is described as the base the others build on.
  - **Fewer rules:** Most of the detailed style guidance was retired. New sections cover reading context, supporting people's own decisions, following social norms, and avoiding unwanted side effects when an agent acts.
  - **Chain of command:** OpenAI sets the outer limits, developers direct their apps inside those limits, and users direct their work inside the developer's limits. Most rules are defaults that can be overridden, and only a few are hard limits.
  - **Small changes:** The document now calls the model "GPT" instead of "the assistant." It is released under CC0 (public domain) on GitHub.
- **Background:** A **spec** like this is the target behavior that training and evaluation aim at. It is a statement of intent, not a guarantee of how the model will act.
- **The catch:** OpenAI says its current models don't fully follow the new spec yet and that it is still testing ways to teach the values behind the rules.
- **Why it matters for you:** If you build on an LLM API, the "chain of command" is how your system prompt and your users' messages are weighed against each other. When the model ignores an instruction, the spec is often the place that explains why.
- **Sources:** [AI/TLDR: OpenAI Model Spec update](https://ai-tldr.dev/releases/openai-model-spec-2026-10-29/) · [The Neuron](https://www.theneuron.ai/digest/everything-that-happened-in-ai-today-october-30-2026/)

**Also noted:** Microsoft put its "Copilot harness" into public preview in Foundry Agent Service on Oct 28. A **harness** is the software loop that runs an agent: it calls the model, runs tools in a sandbox and manages context. The runtime is free until Dec 1, then sandbox compute costs $0.0994 per vCPU-hour ([Microsoft Foundry Blog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/foundry/microsoft-copilot-harness-in-foundry/)).

## Research Highlights

### 2. One algorithm for pretraining, fine-tuning and RL (arXiv:2610.40564)

- **What it is:** A paper posted Oct 29 by Yuda Song, Andrea Zanette and co-authors from CMU and Johns Hopkins, including Ruslan Salakhutdinov and J. Andrew Bagnell. Its title is "Training Foundation Models with a Single Algorithm."
- **The idea, simply:**
  - LLMs are usually trained in stages: pretraining on web text, midtraining on curated data, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on examples, then reinforcement learning (RL). Each stage uses its own algorithm.
  - The authors show that for right-or-wrong rewards, one RL update rule (they call it MaxRL) is mathematically the same as supervised training on the correct answers the model just generated. So RL becomes "maximum likelihood on a moving dataset."
  - If every stage is maximum likelihood, the only real choice is which data to mix in and when. The old staged pipeline is just one schedule, where each data source gets its own block of time.
- **Results:**
  - They trained a 124M-parameter model on reasoning puzzles and a 0.76B model on web text, math and code, both from scratch.
  - At most compute budgets, mixing the data together beat staged training on accuracy. The mixed models also forgot less of their earlier skills and were better starting points for further training.
  - Mixing in even a few percent of midtraining data during RL kept held-out loss down, while staged training made it go up.
- **The catch:** The models are small, and many of the gains are shown only in figures. Mixing helped math but hurt coding accuracy in one setting, and a standard RL method (REINFORCE) still won on some metrics.
- **Why it matters for you:** It is a good example of how much of ML reduces to maximum likelihood (today's ML concept). It also shows that "forgetting" during fine-tuning is partly a data-scheduling problem.
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40564)

### 3. LeCun and Balestriero prove that separately trained models can learn the same world (arXiv:2610.39302)

- **What it is:** A paper posted Oct 29 by Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun, "Proving and Improving the Platonic Representation Hypothesis with JEPAs."
- **The idea, simply:**
  - The **Platonic Representation Hypothesis** is an observation that vision models and language models, trained separately, end up with similar internal representations as they grow. Until now it was an empirical pattern without a proof.
  - The authors prove it for **JEPAs** (Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures), models trained to make the embeddings of two related inputs match. If the two inputs are two "views" of the same scene, such as an image and its caption, encoders trained on different modalities learn the same representation, up to a rotation.
  - They argue that next-token prediction is a JEPA with weaker views (two parts of the same document), which would explain why LLMs only line up with vision models at very large scale.
- **Results (self-reported):**
  - Good views needed 42x fewer training samples than next-token prediction on the same text to reach the same alignment with vision models.
  - A 109M-parameter text JEPA aligned with the DINOv2 and CLIP image encoders better than every language model they tested, up to 72B parameters.
  - Rotating one encoder's space onto the other gave an image captioner with no paired training data (CIDEr 0.838 on COCO), and 64% top-5 zero-shot accuracy on ImageNet-100, vs at most 56% for language models up to 8B.
- **The catch:** The proof depends on having "good views," which can be hard to get in practice. The results are new and haven't been reproduced by others.
- **Why it matters for you:** It is a clean example of why self-supervised learning (today's deep learning concept) matters. What a model learns depends heavily on which pairs of examples you tell it are "the same."
- **Source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39302)

## Tools & Libraries

### 4. Transformers 5.22 lets you LoRA-train FP8 models

- **What happened:** Hugging Face released Transformers v5.22.0 on Oct 29, plus a v5.21.1 patch.
- **Key details:**
  - **FP8 adapter training:** Models stored in FP8 (8-bit floating point) can now be fine-tuned with adapters such as LoRA through the PEFT library. Before, you usually had to load a higher-precision copy first.
  - **New models:** Mistral Large 4 (the 1-trillion-parameter open model covered yesterday) and NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe speech model.
  - **MoE changes:** Lower memory use in the backward pass for mixture-of-experts models. Expert-parallel code moved to its own file, which is a breaking change if you import it directly.
  - **Patch:** v5.21.1 fixes a bug that broke loading remote code in vLLM.
- **Background:** **FP8** stores each number in 8 bits instead of 16, halving memory. An **adapter** is a small set of new weights trained on top of a frozen model. LoRA is the most common one (see today's LLM concept).
- **Why it matters for you:** This lowers the memory you need to fine-tune a big model, which is often the bottleneck on a student budget. Pin your `transformers` version in `requirements.txt`, since minor releases do include breaking changes.
- **Sources:** [Releases.sh: Transformers releases](https://releases.sh/hugging-face/transformers-releases) · [Releasebot: Hugging Face updates](https://releasebot.io/updates/huggingface)

### 5. A trojanized Claude Code extension planted SSH backdoors through Cursor's marketplace

- **What happened:** On Oct 29, version 2.1.313 of Anthropic's official Claude Code extension appeared on the Cursor Marketplace with malicious code added. It was live from about 2:27 a.m. to 1:35 p.m. PT, roughly 11 hours.
- **Key details:**
  - **What it did:** About 66 lines of code were added to the extension. They put an attacker's SSH key into `~/.ssh/authorized_keys`, tried to turn on SSH, opened a reverse tunnel, and set up tasks to survive a restart.
  - **Data sent out:** Hostname, username, OS, public IP, Git email, and the *names* (not values) of up to 40 environment variables containing words like KEY, TOKEN or SECRET.
  - **Scope:** Cursor says at most 5,000 users were affected and it will contact them. There's no evidence that the Microsoft VS Code Marketplace or Open VSX copies were affected. Anthropic published a clean version, 2.1.314.
  - **Unknown:** How the attacker pushed an update under Anthropic's real publisher name hasn't been determined.
- **Background:** This is a **supply-chain attack**: instead of attacking you directly, the attacker poisons software you trust and let auto-update install it.
- **Why it matters for you:** If you use Cursor, check your extension version and `authorized_keys`, and rotate API keys if you were affected. In general, editor extensions run with your full user permissions, so keep the list short and know where each one came from.
- **Sources:** [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/10/official-claude-code-vs-code-extension.html) · [Microsoft Security Blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/10/30/inside-malicious-claude-code-branded-cursor-extension-persistent-ssh-backdoor/)

## Industry & Policy

### 6. Four AI companies agree on one robots.txt "no training" signal

- **What happened:** On Oct 30, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic said they will honor a shared robots.txt token, `__usage_ai-training`, that tells AI companies a site's content shouldn't be used for training.
- **Key details:**
  - **How it works:** A site adds a group with `User-Agent: __usage_ai-training` and `Disallow: /` (with optional `Allow:` exceptions). It describes how content may be *used*, not whether it can be crawled.
  - **Timelines:** Anthropic says it has started implementing it. Microsoft starts Feb 1, 2027, Google targets 2027, and OpenAI says "in the coming months." Meta, Amazon and Apple made no commitment.
  - **Training vs grounding:** The draft covers changing a model's weights. It does not cover **grounding**, where a chatbot fetches a page live to answer a question. Google will treat the token as also blocking Gemini grounding unless a site explicitly allows it.
  - **Status:** It is an individual IETF draft written by Anthropic's Kevin Kelley, with no official standing, and it is voluntary.
- **Why it matters for you:** If you build web-scraped datasets, robots.txt preferences are becoming a basic part of data ethics and compliance. Logging when and under which rules you collected each page is good practice.
- **Source:** [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-microsoft-openai-and-anthropic-back-one-ai-training-opt-out/)

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

**In one sentence:** Maximum likelihood estimation picks the model parameters that make the data you actually observed as probable as possible.

**Intuition:** You find a coin and flip it 10 times: 7 heads. Which coin is most likely to have produced that? A fair coin could, but a coin that lands heads 70% of the time makes that exact result more likely. MLE formalizes "pick the explanation that best fits what you saw."

**Formula / Tiny example:** For 7 heads and 3 tails, the likelihood of heads-probability p is L(p) = p⁷(1 − p)³.
- L(0.5) = 0.5¹⁰ ≈ 0.00098
- L(0.6) ≈ 0.00179
- L(0.7) ≈ 0.00222 (the highest)
- L(0.8) ≈ 0.00168

So the MLE is p = 0.7, the observed fraction. In practice we maximize the **log**-likelihood (sums are easier than products), or equivalently minimize the negative log-likelihood, which is exactly the cross-entropy loss used to train classifiers and LLMs.
```python
import numpy as np
p = np.linspace(0.01, 0.99, 99)
log_lik = 7*np.log(p) + 3*np.log(1 - p)
print(p[np.argmax(log_lik)])   # 0.7
```

**When it's used:** Logistic regression, fitting distributions, next-token prediction in LLMs, and, per story 2, even a form of RL.

**Common pitfalls:** With little data, MLE overfits: 3 heads in 3 flips gives p = 1.0, which says tails is impossible. Regularization or a prior (as in MAP estimation) fixes this. Also, likelihood values themselves are tiny and not probabilities of the parameters.

**In today's news:** Story 2 shows a common RL update is maximum likelihood on the model's own correct answers, so all training stages can share one loss.

### LLMs: Fine-Tuning with LoRA

**In one sentence:** LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) fine-tunes a large model by freezing its weights and training two small matrices whose product is added to each chosen weight matrix.

**Intuition:** Instead of repainting a whole house to change its look, you add a few removable decals. The house (the base model) stays the same, the decals (the adapter) are cheap to make, and you can swap them for different tasks.

**Formula / Tiny example:** A layer has weight W of size 4096 × 4096, which is 16,777,216 numbers. LoRA learns B (4096 × r) and A (r × 4096) and uses W + (α/r)·BA. With rank r = 8, that's 2 × 4096 × 8 = 65,536 trainable numbers, about 0.39% of the full matrix.
```python
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model
config = LoraConfig(r=8, lora_alpha=16, target_modules=["q_proj", "v_proj"], lora_dropout=0.05)
model = get_peft_model(base_model, config)
model.print_trainable_parameters()   # typically well under 1% trainable
```

**When it's used:** Customizing open models for a company's style or domain, training many task-specific adapters on one base model, and fine-tuning on a single GPU. QLoRA combines it with a quantized base model.

**Common pitfalls:** Expecting LoRA to add new knowledge the base model lacks. It is best at adjusting behavior and format. Also, a learning rate that's too high, or too few target layers, can make results look worse than full fine-tuning when the real issue is settings.

**In today's news:** Story 4: Transformers 5.22 lets you train LoRA adapters directly on FP8 models, cutting memory further.

### Deep Learning: Self-Supervised and Contrastive Learning

**In one sentence:** Self-supervised learning creates training signals from unlabeled data itself, and contrastive learning does it by pulling embeddings of matching pairs together and pushing non-matching ones apart.

**Intuition:** A child learns that a photo of a dog and the word "dog" go together without anyone grading a quiz, just from seeing them together many times and seeing them apart from "cat" and "car."

**Formula / Tiny example:** A common contrastive loss (InfoNCE) is a softmax over similarities, divided by a **temperature** τ. An image's embedding has cosine similarity 0.9 with its true caption and 0.3 and 0.1 with two wrong captions.
- With τ = 1: logits 0.9, 0.3, 0.1. P(correct) = e^0.9 / (e^0.9 + e^0.3 + e^0.1) ≈ 0.50, loss = −ln(0.50) ≈ 0.69.
- With τ = 0.1: logits 9, 3, 1. P(correct) ≈ 0.997, loss ≈ 0.003.

Lower temperature sharpens the softmax, so small differences in similarity matter more.
```python
import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
sims = torch.tensor([[0.9, 0.3, 0.1]])          # positive is index 0
loss = F.cross_entropy(sims / 0.1, torch.tensor([0]))
```

**When it's used:** CLIP (images and text), DINOv2 (images), sentence-embedding models for search and RAG, and pretraining when labels are scarce.

**Common pitfalls:** **Collapse**, where every input maps to the same embedding so the loss looks fine but nothing is learned. Negatives or extra regularization prevent it. Also, weak "views" or augmentations teach the model the wrong notion of "same."

**In today's news:** Story 3 proves that JEPAs, a self-supervised method, trained on different modalities learn the same representation when given good views.
