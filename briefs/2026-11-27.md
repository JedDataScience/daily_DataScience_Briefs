## AI & Machine Learning

### 1. Ant Group's Ling-3.2-air: a big open model that runs like a small one

- **What happened:** On Tuesday, Nov 24, Ant Group's inclusionAI team released the weights of Ling-3.2-air, a model that reads text, images and video, under the MIT license.
- **Key details:**
  - 124B total parameters, but only 5.5B are *active* for each token (about 4.4%). The model has 512 routed experts plus 1 shared expert, and picks 8 per token.
  - Its attention is mostly *linear*: 35 Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) layers and 7 Gated MLA layers in a 5:1 pattern. Only the 7 MLA layers keep a growing KV cache, so long contexts are cheaper to hold. The recommended setup serves a 256K-token context on four 141 GB-class GPUs.
  - Self-reported gains over its predecessor, Ling-3.0-flash-VL: AutomationBench-AA (office-task automation) rose from 15.7% to 61%, and Terminal-Bench 4.0 from 0% to 27%.
  - Day-one serving recipes for SGLang and vLLM (via an inclusionAI fork), and 25 quantized versions are already listed on Hugging Face.
- **Background:** "MIT license" is one of the most permissive open-source licenses: you can use, modify and sell products built on the weights as long as you keep the copyright notice. "Active parameters" are the ones that actually run for a given token in a mixture-of-experts model, so they set the compute cost, while total parameters set the memory cost.
- **The catch:** every benchmark number is Ant's own. Even its own legal-task result looks shaky: on Harvey LAB-AA, the share of tasks fully passed drops from 8.3% to 0.8% once a hallucination check is applied. The model card also lists "128B params" in its header against 124B in its own table.
- **Why it matters for you:** this is a clear example of where open models are going: huge total size, small active size, and hybrid linear attention to keep memory flat. When you read a model card, check active vs total parameters and the KV-cache design before you assume you can (or can't) run it.
- **Source:** [Hugging Face: inclusionAI/Ling-3.2-air](https://huggingface.co/inclusionAI/Ling-3.2-air), [TechNode](https://technode.com/2026/11/25/ant-releases-ling-3-2-air-multimodal-model-under-mit-license/)

## Research Highlights

### 3. "Chance level" doesn't prove a model forgot your data (arXiv:2611.33525)

- **What it is:** "Deletion You Can Check: Auditing LLM Unlearning," by Mayank Gulati and Gerhard Wunder, listed in cs.LG on Nov 26.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - To check whether a model "forgot" some records, people often run a *membership-inference* test: can an attacker tell the deleted records apart from records the model never saw? An ROC-AUC near 0.5 (a coin flip) is read as "forgotten."
  - The authors point out that this only works if the "never seen" comparison records really look like the deleted ones. Otherwise the test measures differences between datasets, not memory.
  - They test this on two benchmarks (ArxivMIA and BookMIA) and two small models (Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen2.5-1.5B), using an exactly retrained model, which by definition never saw the data, as the reference.
- **Results:**
  - On ArxivMIA, the retrained reference landed inside the chance band, as it should. On BookMIA it scored 0.122, so the same test would wrongly say a model that never saw the data had not forgotten it.
  - Swapping in comparison records drawn from the same pool moved that reference from 0.257 to 0.523, which supports the mismatch explanation.
  - On ArxivMIA with Llama, none of the tested settings of three popular unlearning methods (gradient ascent with retain descent, NPO-RT and SCRUB) passed a combined check of forgetting, change beyond normal variation, and keeping utility.
- **Why it matters for you:** a metric means nothing without the right baseline. Before you trust a "0.5 means random" reading, ask: random *compared to what*? The same logic applies to any A/B comparison where the two groups come from different sources.
- **Source:** [arXiv cs.LG new submissions, Nov 26](https://arxiv.org/list/cs.LG/new) (arXiv:2611.33525)

### 4. Your Gaussian process may need more than double precision (arXiv:2611.33647)

- **What it is:** "The Danger of Double Precision: Working Precision as a Hidden Constraint in Gaussian Process Maximum Likelihood," by Thomas A. Scott.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - Gaussian processes (GPs) are fitted by tuning kernel settings (hyperparameters) to maximize a likelihood, and that maths needs the inverse of a covariance matrix. With smooth kernels and tightly clustered inputs, that matrix is nearly singular (see today's ML concept).
  - The author refits the same GP at many precisions and compares against a high-precision reference. Fits only settle once the number of bits exceeds log2 of the matrix's *condition number*, about 70 bits in the main example, while a standard float64 carries 53.
  - In double precision, the Cholesky factorization fails for some settings, and the optimizer silently avoids them. That hidden constraint, not the data, decides the answer.
- **Results:**
  - Across 43 kernels, designs and settings, the bits needed track the log condition number plus an offset.
  - At the loosest tolerance, double precision was enough for a Matérn-1.5 kernel but not for the squared-exponential kernel on clustered designs.
  - The double-precision fits overstated uncertainty near the cluster and could stall active learning. Raising the likelihood to 80 bits recovered the reference on every fixed design tested. Adding a fixed "jitter" to the diagonal avoided crashes but biased the predictions.
- **Why it matters for you:** if you use scikit-learn's `GaussianProcessRegressor` or Bayesian optimization, warnings about ill-conditioning aren't noise. Check the condition number of your kernel matrix and be wary when many points sit very close together.
- **Source:** [arXiv cs.LG new submissions, Nov 26](https://arxiv.org/list/cs.LG/new) (arXiv:2611.33647)

### 5. Fine-tuning hides old behavior fast but erases it slowly (arXiv:2611.33302)

- **What it is:** "Two Timescales of Forgetting in Fine-tuning of Language Models: Fast Behavioral Suppression and Slow Representational Drift," by Yilin Luo, Jin Hwa Lee, Andrew M. Saxe and Antonio Sclocchi.
- **The idea, simply:**
  - The authors pretrain transformers on a controlled artificial grammar (the Random Hierarchy Model), then fine-tune them on only some of its rules.
  - The model quickly *stops producing* the dropped rules. Its internal representations of those rules, however, stay intact at first and fade much later, through slow drift in the weights.
  - They see the same gap between behavior and representation in a natural-language setup.
- **Why it matters for you:** "the model no longer does X" is not the same as "the model no longer knows X." This connects directly to story 3: an output-level test can make forgetting look more complete than it is. See today's deep learning concept.
- **Source:** [arXiv cs.LG new submissions, Nov 26](https://arxiv.org/list/cs.LG/new) (arXiv:2611.33302)

**Also noted:** CovNovBench (arXiv:2611.33732) tests six time-series foundation models on 88,476 series where future covariates fall outside the historical range, and finds no model consistently reliable; a new concept-drift benchmark framework (arXiv:2611.33424) stress-tests 10 drift detectors on 16 datasets ([arXiv cs.LG](https://arxiv.org/list/cs.LG/new)).

## Tools & Libraries

### 6. Magnitude runs mixture-of-experts models bigger than your GPU

- **What happened:** On Nov 24, the open-source inference engine Magnitude relaunched with a design that splits model weights across three tiers (GPU memory, system RAM and NVMe SSD) and moves them during generation.
- **Key details:**
  - It only works for mixture-of-experts models. In the Qwen model it tests, each token uses 10 of 512 routed experts (about 2%), so the shared layers and most-used experts stay on the GPU and the rest are streamed from RAM or SSD with prefetching.
  - Developer-reported decode speeds at 2-bit, 64K context with speculative decoding: 41 tokens/s for a 66 GB model on an RTX 3090 (24 GB) with 64 GB RAM, and 17 tokens/s on an RTX 3060 (12 GB) with 32 GB RAM.
  - Written in Rust, runs on CUDA, Vulkan (AMD/Intel), Metal (Apple) or CPU, and offers an OpenAI-compatible API. The README says Apache-2.0, though an open issue flags one component marked "UNLICENSED."
- **The catch:** these are decode-only numbers. Prompt processing and time to first token aren't covered, and one user on an RTX 5080 reported speeds 1.8–3x *slower* than Ollama on the same file. Dense models don't benefit.
- **Why it matters for you:** it's a hands-on lesson in memory hierarchies. Knowing that only ~2% of experts run per token explains why this works at all, and it's the same reason "active parameters" matter in story 1.
- **Source:** [The Neuron](https://www.theneuron.ai/news/magnitude-uses-your-ssd-to-run-ai-models-bigger-than-your-memory/), [Aliteq](https://aliteq.com/magnitude-run-big-moe-models-from-ssd)

**Also noted:** ggml-org and AMD launched a public llama.cpp benchmark dashboard on Hugging Face (Nov 26) that runs weekly llama-bench tests on a Radeon 8060S and a Radeon AI Pro R9700S, with raw results in a public bucket ([Data Phoenix](https://dataphoenix.info/news/llama-cpp-amd-benchmark-dashboard-launch)).

## Concepts of the Day

### Machine Learning: Gaussian Process Regression

**In one sentence:** Gaussian process (GP) regression predicts a value at a new point as a weighted blend of nearby training points, and also gives an uncertainty for every prediction.

**Intuition:** imagine guessing the temperature in a town between two weather stations. You trust the nearer station more, and you're more confident when you're close to a station than when you're far from both. A GP formalizes that "nearby points are similar" idea with a *kernel*, a function that scores how similar two inputs are.

**Formula / Tiny example:** a common kernel is the squared-exponential (RBF): k(x, x') = exp(−(x − x')² / (2ℓ²)), where ℓ is the length scale. Take ℓ = 1, training points x = 0 and 1 with y = 1 and 2, no noise.
- Kernel matrix K = [[1, 0.607], [0.607, 1]], since exp(−0.5) ≈ 0.607.
- At x* = 0.5, the similarities are k* = [0.882, 0.882].
- Mean = k*ᵀ K⁻¹ y ≈ 1.648, and variance = 1 − k*ᵀ K⁻¹ k* ≈ 0.030 (small, because x* is close to both points).

```python
from sklearn.gaussian_process import GaussianProcessRegressor
from sklearn.gaussian_process.kernels import RBF
gp = GaussianProcessRegressor(kernel=RBF(1.0), optimizer=None).fit([[0],[1]], [1,2])
mean, std = gp.predict([[0.5]], return_std=True)   # mean ≈ 1.648
```

**When it's used:** small-data regression, Bayesian optimization (tuning hyperparameters with few, expensive trials), spatial statistics, and active learning.

**Common pitfalls:** (1) Points that are almost identical make K nearly singular. Move the second point from 1 to 0.01 and K's condition number jumps to about 40,000, and in story 4 much worse cases needed ~70 bits of precision. (2) Exact GPs cost O(n³) in the number of points, so they don't scale past a few thousand without approximations.

**In today's news:** story 4 shows that this ill-conditioning can silently change the fitted hyperparameters.

### LLMs: Machine Unlearning

**In one sentence:** machine unlearning means removing the influence of specific training data from a trained model without retraining it from scratch.

**Intuition:** retraining from scratch is like rewriting a whole textbook to remove one paragraph. It's correct but expensive. Unlearning tries to erase that paragraph with targeted edits, and the hard part is proving you really got it all.

**Formula / Tiny example:** the gold standard is a model retrained without the "forget set." Approximate methods, such as *gradient ascent*, instead push the loss on the forget set *up* while a "retain" loss keeps everything else working. To audit, you run a membership-inference test: score each record by how "familiar" the model finds it, then compute the ROC-AUC between forget-set records and records it never saw.
- Forget-set scores: [0.9, 0.7, 0.6, 0.4]; never-seen scores: [0.8, 0.5, 0.3, 0.2].
- AUC = share of (forget, never-seen) pairs where the forget record scores higher = (4 + 3 + 3 + 2) / 16 = 0.75. That is well above 0.5, so the model still "remembers."

**When it's used:** honoring data-deletion requests (such as GDPR's right to erasure), removing copyrighted or toxic content, and removing hazardous knowledge.

**Common pitfalls:** (1) Treating AUC ≈ 0.5 as proof without checking that the never-seen records come from the same distribution (story 3). (2) Checking only outputs: a model can stop *saying* something while still *representing* it internally (story 5).

**In today's news:** stories 3 and 5; story 7 shows why deletion guarantees matter in practice.

### Deep Learning: Catastrophic Forgetting

**In one sentence:** catastrophic forgetting is when a neural network trained on a new task loses much of its ability on an earlier task.

**Intuition:** the same weights store every skill. Fine-tuning moves them toward the new task, and nothing tells gradient descent to protect the old one. It's like repainting a shared wall for a new tenant and covering the previous tenant's mural.

**Formula / Tiny example:** one classic fix is *Elastic Weight Consolidation* (EWC). It adds a penalty that keeps weights that mattered for the old task near their old values: penalty = (λ/2) Σᵢ Fᵢ (θᵢ − θ*ᵢ)², where θ* are the old weights and Fᵢ estimates how important weight i was.
- Two weights with θ* = [1, 1], importance F = [2, 0.1], new θ = [1.5, 3], λ = 1.
- Penalty = 0.5 × (2 × 0.5² + 0.1 × 2²) = 0.5 × (0.5 + 0.4) = 0.45. The important weight moved only 0.5 but contributes more than the unimportant one that moved 2.

```python
penalty = (lam / 2) * sum((F[n] * (p - old[n])**2).sum() for n, p in model.named_parameters())
loss = task_loss + penalty
```

**When it's used:** continual learning, fine-tuning LLMs on narrow data, and on-device models that keep learning. The simplest fix is often *replay*: mix some old-task data into the new training batches.

**Common pitfalls:** (1) Evaluating only on the new task after fine-tuning, so you never see what broke. Always keep a held-out test set from the original task. (2) Assuming that low old-task accuracy means the knowledge is gone. Story 5 shows it can still sit in the representations.

**In today's news:** story 5 splits forgetting into a fast behavioral stage and a slow representational stage.
